# How to choose an AI workforce platform: 7 questions to ask before you commit

Every AI platform now has the same pricing page: named AI teammates, a credit balance, a free trial. The labels have converged. What's underneath them has not — and the difference shows up in your second week, not in the demo.

Before you commit to any of them, ask seven questions. None of them is about which product has the nicest landing page. All of them are about what happens after you've paid.

## What is an AI workforce platform supposed to do?

It should put pre-staffed AI colleagues to work in your existing tools, with finished deliverables landing somewhere you can review them — not hand you a toolkit for building agents yourself. Those are two different products, and most confusion in this category is people buying one while expecting the other.

If you enjoy building workflows — connecting nodes, wiring triggers, owning the machinery — a builder platform is a legitimate choice. Lindy and Relevance AI are the serious ones, and Lindy's approval features are genuinely well done. But if you want the outcome without becoming the architect, look for platforms where the team arrives assembled: you describe the result, colleagues divide the work, and you review what they produce.

## Will it work with the tools you already use?

The honest test is day one, not a demo. If a platform needs hours of integration setup before any work happens, the setup is the product — you've bought another thing to maintain.

Ask specifically: how many integrations are connected out of the box, and can you scope which tools each colleague can touch? Kohai connects around 1,000 apps on day one, with per-agent tool scoping, so your Sales Lead can reach the CRM without reaching your accounting. Whichever platform you choose, "connected before I briefed anyone" is the standard to hold.

## What happens when it touches something sensitive?

Some work should never run unattended: sends to customers, payments, anything with outside impact. The platform's answer to this should be an approval gate — the colleague pauses, shows you the draft, and waits.

This is table stakes, and credit where it's due: Lindy states it plainly — anything with outside impact waits for approval. Ask every platform you evaluate the same question, and be specific: does the gate apply per action, can you set the rules yourself, and is the pause visible in the product or buried in logs?

## What models run it, and where does your data go?

Most buyers never ask this question, and most vendors never volunteer the answer. You should ask anyway, because the model layer determines both your privacy posture and your cost structure.

Kohai's answer is on the record: open-weight models only — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — from certified no-training providers. That means your client notes, invoices, and pipeline data are not training material, and per-tenant isolation keeps them separated from every other tenant's. Other platforms may match parts of this, but few publish their model stack at all. A vendor who can't answer in one sentence is giving you an answer.

## What will it cost in a normal month?

Token-billed AI tools make budgeting a guessing game — the bill scales with usage you don't control until the invoice arrives. Credit-based platforms invert this: you buy a fixed monthly allowance and can see what typical work costs before you assign it.

Kohai's entry plan is $29.99 for 12,000 credits, with a 7-day free trial, and a blog post costs 20–50 credits while a deck runs 100–300. That concreteness is the thing to test on any platform: if you can't estimate what a week of real work costs before you commit, you're not choosing a plan, you're renting a meter.

## Can it ship finished work, or just conversation?

A chat answer is a starting point. A deliverable is something you'd send to a client, hand to your accountant, or publish. The platforms worth your money produce the second kind, and put finished work somewhere you can find it again.

Kohai's Library collects deliverables — reports, drafts, follow-ups — and its App Studio ships deployed software, not mockups. Whatever you evaluate, ask to see where finished work lands and what "done" looks like on a real task. If the answer ends at a chat window, you're hiring a conversationalist.

## What happens when the trial ends?

A 7-day trial is only informative if real work happens inside it. Before signing up, decide what you'll delegate on day one — a Monday report, an invoice chase, a support queue — and judge the platform on that task, not on its welcome message.

The platforms that survive your second week are the ones that answered all seven questions before you asked. That's the actual selection criterion: judge every platform on its answers, not its landing page. Everything else is craft.

| Question | What good looks like | Red flag |
|---|---|---|
| Tools | Connected day one, scoped per agent | "Integrations take an afternoon" |

| Models & data | Model stack published, no-training certified | "We use the best models" |
| Cost | Fixed credits, visible per-task costs | Usage bill you can't predict |
| Output | Deliverables in a library, software deployed | Everything ends in chat |
