# How to triage support tickets with an AI colleague (without losing the refund button)

The support queue is the one part of your business that answers to nobody's calendar. It fills while you sleep, while you're in a client call, while you're finally doing the work you actually sell. And triage — reading, sorting, deciding what matters — is the part that eats the time, long before anyone writes a reply.

Here's the version that stops eating your week: an AI colleague reads every ticket, sorts them, drafts the replies, and hands you only the ones that need a human decision. You keep the send button on anything sensitive. Here's how to set it up.

## Why triage is the right first support job for AI

Most support work is sorting, not deciding. "Where's my order?" is a lookup. "How do I export my data?" is a doc link. Maybe one ticket in ten needs judgment — an angry customer, an edge case, a refund request.

That ratio is exactly why triage works as a first assignment. Your AI colleague does the volume work: classify, match to a known answer, draft. You do the judgment work, and only that. Nothing goes out the door without the process you define.

## The brief is one message

You don't build a workflow or write a prompt. You brief the way you'd brief a new hire on their first day:

> "Handle first-pass triage on the support inbox. Classify each ticket: question, bug, billing, or escalation. Draft replies for questions using our help docs. For bugs, collect the details and flag me. Anything about refunds, cancellations, or an upset customer — draft it, but it waits for my approval."

That's the whole job description. The Support Lead takes it from there, and the first sorted queue is in your Library today — not after a two-week implementation project.

## What happens to each ticket

The division of labor looks like this:

| Ticket type | Your AI colleague does | You do |
|---|---|---|
| Common question | Drafts a reply from your docs | Approve or edit, then send |
| Bug report | Pulls the details, reproduces the steps, files it | Decide priority |
| Billing / refund | Drafts the response, holds at the approval gate | Approve, edit, or decline |
| Upset customer | Flags it, summarizes the history | Write (or approve) the reply yourself |
| Feature request | Logs and tags it | Review the list monthly |

Two things worth noticing in that table. First, the AI colleague never sends the sensitive stuff on its own — refunds and cancellations wait at an approval gate until you say go. Second, the drafts still come from *your* docs and *your* policies, so the replies sound like your company, not a call center.

## The tool access question

Your colleague needs to read the inbox and search the help docs — and that's all it should need. This is where per-agent tool scoping matters: each hire only touches the tools you allow. The Support Lead sees your helpdesk and documentation; it does not see your Stripe dashboard, your code repo, or your customer database unless you grant it.

That scoping is also what makes the approval gate meaningful. A colleague that can read tickets but not move money can draft a refund response; it can't issue one. Trust comes from the boundary, not from hope.

## What your customers' data — and yours — is doing

Support tickets are the most sensitive text your business produces: names, order details, sometimes payment questions, sometimes frustration written in an unguarded moment. Where that text goes matters.

Kohai runs exclusively on open-weight models, and the inference providers are certified not to train on your data. Open-weight models only — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — from certified no-training providers. Your tickets aren't becoming someone's training corpus, and your customers' words aren't leaking into someone else's autocomplete. For a solo operator, that's the same promise enterprises get, at a price that isn't enterprise.

## What it costs to try it

"First AI hire: $29.99/month, 12,000 credits, 7-day free trial." That's the entry tier, and the 7-day trial is long enough to run real tickets through it — not a demo queue, your actual inbox. If the sorted queue and drafted replies don't save you the five hours a week that support triage typically costs a solo operator, cancel and you're out nothing.

## Your first week

Day one: brief the Support Lead, connect the helpdesk, and let it sort the existing backlog. Day two: review its drafts and tighten the brief where its answers miss your voice — every brief gets one revision pass, the same way you'd correct a new hire's first week. Day three on: your morning routine is one approval pass instead of forty unread emails. Recurring schedules take it further: set the triage to run on a schedule or on new-ticket triggers, and it holds without you re-briefing it weekly.

The queue never goes to zero — but the part of it that needs *you* gets small enough to handle before lunch. That's the whole pitch. Not magic, just the sorting done by something that never lets the inbox pile up on a Friday afternoon.
