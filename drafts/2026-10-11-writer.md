# Why your AI bill doesn't fall when models get cheaper

Running a language model keeps getting cheaper. Open-weight model generations compete with each other on the price of doing the same piece of work, and the cost of inference trends down over time. Your AI bill hasn't followed that curve — and whether it ever will depends on one thing: where you sit in the stack.

The distinction matters because "AI is getting cheaper" and "my AI spend is getting cheaper" are different claims. The first is about the technology. The second is about who captures the savings, and that's a property of the platform you buy from — not of the models underneath it.

## Why is AI still expensive if the models are getting cheaper?

Because the platform you subscribe to sits between you and the model, and that middle layer decides what falling costs mean for your invoice. When a platform buys its intelligence at retail from a closed frontier provider and resells it by the token, falling wholesale prices are margin before they are ever savings. Whether the platform keeps them or passes them down is a business decision — and one most buyers never think to ask about.

Metering hides the trend too. Token-based bills charge for computation — the prompts, the retries, the internal steps — so even as the raw cost of a unit of work falls, your usage grows to fill the space, and the number on the invoice stays a number on the invoice. Falling costs and a flat-or-rising bill can coexist for years without anyone lying to you.

## Where do the savings actually go?

The two stack shapes in this category handle falling model costs differently:

| | Closed frontier stack | Open-weight stack |
|---|---|---|
| What the platform pays for | A rented frontier API, priced per token | Open-weight inference, competed on price |
| Who competes on your behalf | Nobody above the platform | Every provider running the same published weights |
| Where falling model costs land | With the platform | With the buyer — held prices or more capacity per plan |
| What you can verify | The invoice | The model stack, named in one sentence |

The third row is the whole difference. In a closed stack, the buyer can't see the input cost, so there's nothing to check the bill against — the meter is the truth. In an open stack, the input cost is public and competed, which makes "our plan economics track the cost of running the work" a checkable claim instead of a hope. That's not an argument that open-weight platforms are cheaper on any given day. It's an argument about which architecture is even capable of passing a falling cost curve through to the buyer.

## What does open-weight competition change for the buyer?

Start with the mechanics. Open-weight models — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — publish their weights, so any inference provider can run them, and those providers compete on price and on trust. That competition is the transmission mechanism: it's what converts falling model costs into something a buyer can see, rather than something a platform quietly keeps.

It also changes what a credit is worth at the top end. Kohai prices the work, not the machinery: a blog post runs 20–50 credits, a deck 100–300, and the same pool extends upward — "App Studio ships deployed software, not mockups." A credit buys a drafted document at the low end of that scale and a running application at the high end. The claim a buyer should care about isn't that credits get cheaper every quarter — it's that the platform's economics leave room for prices to hold or capacity to grow as inference gets cheaper, because there's no rented-API markup forcing the opposite.

## What should you ask a vendor about falling costs?

Four questions separate a stack that passes savings through from one that absorbs them. Ask them before the trial, not after the invoice:

| Question | A real answer | A shrug |
|---|---|---|
| Which models run the work? | Named in one sentence | "The best available" |
| What am I billed for besides the deliverable? | Nothing — retries are the platform's problem | "Usage varies" |
| What does a task cost before I assign it? | A published per-task range | A monthly total and a meter |
| What happens to your costs as model prices fall? | A stated position on prices or capacity | Usually, no answer at all |

The last row does the real work. Most buyers never ask it, so most vendors have never had to answer it — and a vendor who has to improvise on the cost-trend question is telling you where falling prices go. A vendor running an open-weight stack can usually answer in a sentence, because competition at the inference layer is the thing their whole pricing model is built on.

## How do you test this before committing?

Use the trial as the measurement, not the demo. The 7-day free trial puts a first deliverable in your Library the same day — brief one recurring task, read its credit quote before the work runs, then read it again the following week. If the price of a task is visible before the work and stable after it, you're buying from a stack where the economics face you instead of hiding behind a meter.

The direction of AI costs isn't a secret; inference is the layer of the stack getting cheaper fastest. Whether any of that reaches your bill is a property of the platform — and it's checkable in one week, with one task.
