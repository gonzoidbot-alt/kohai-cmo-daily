# Per-Seat vs Flat-Rate AI Pricing: What Your Bill Says About the Architecture

The short version: if an AI platform charges per seat, the bill multiplies every time you add a function. If it charges a flat rate with unlimited seats, the bill barely moves. That difference isn't a pricing strategy — it's a consequence of the models underneath.

Most businesses evaluating AI platforms this year will compare two numbers: the per-user price and the monthly minimum. The trap is assuming those numbers describe the same thing. They don't. One reflects a licensing cost that scales with headcount. The other reflects an inference cost that scales with work done. The architecture determines which one you get.

## What per-seat pricing actually buys

When an AI platform charges per seat, it's usually because the platform runs frontier models — the ones where the provider pays a licensing premium per token, per user, or per API call. The per-seat price is a proxy for that variable cost. Each team member you add means another allocation of model access, and the provider recovers the licensing overhead by charging for the seat, not the work.

This structure creates an odd incentive: you pay the same monthly price for a seat whether that person drafts one email a day or a hundred. The platform's cost is driven by the number of users, not the value of the output. For a solo founder adding two or three AI capabilities, this works fine — the seat count is low, and the per-person price feels reasonable. The economics shift when you need five or six functions running simultaneously. At that point, the pricing model becomes a tax on coverage: adding a support function means adding a support seat on top of the sales seat, the content seat, the ops seat. The bill grows with headcount, not with output.

There's nothing deceptive about this model. It's the SaaS standard, and for platforms built on per-user licensing costs, it's the honest price. But it is worth understanding before you sign up: a per-seat plan means your cost floor rises every time you add a role.

## How flat-rate plans on open-weight models work differently

Open-weight models are published with their weights available for anyone to host. That changes the cost structure at the foundation level: there's no per-user licensing fee to pass through. The cost of running the model is the inference compute — the server time per query — and that cost is driven by usage volume, not seat count. A platform that runs on open-weight models can structure its pricing around work delivered rather than users provisioned.

Kōhai's pricing is a case in point: every plan — Free ($0, limited time), $20/mo, $100/mo Pro, $200/mo Max — includes unlimited team seats. Adding a Strategy Lead and a Customer Support Lead does not increase the monthly bill. The credit pool covers the work they both produce, and credits are consumed by task size, not by headcount. A blog post costs 20–50 credits whether the Marketing Lead drafts it or the Operations Lead cross-posts it. The bill changes only when your work volume changes — not when your team shape changes.

**"Open-weight models only — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — from certified no-training providers."**

That one line from Kōhai's architecture is why the pricing works the way it does. Without per-user licensing costs to pass through, the platform can decouple headcount from the monthly bill.

## What this means for a solo founder building a team

The practical difference shows up in month three. You started with one function — maybe content or sales outreach — on a per-seat plan at $30/mo. By month three you need support triage, Monday reporting, and invoice follow-up. With a per-user platform, that's now $90–$120/mo (3–4 seats), and it keeps compounding as you add functions. With a flat-rate plan on an open-weight platform, it's whatever you paid in month one — plus whatever the additional tasks consume from the same credit pool.

This is not a claim that one model is universally better. Per-seat pricing has a real advantage: it's predictable per person, which matters for budgeting headcount in a structured team. For an established team of five people who all need the same tool, per-user pricing is transparent and fair. Flat-rate plans are better suited to the opposite case: one person who needs five functions, or a small team whose role coverage changes week to week. The pricing model should fit the shape of the work, not the other way around.

## What to ask before you compare plans

When you evaluate an AI platform for your team, three questions tell you more than any price table:

**How does the bill change when I add a new function?** If the answer is "you need another seat," you are in a per-headcount model. If the answer is "your credit burn may go up, but the plan doesn't," you are in a work-volume model.

**Is the model architecture published?** Platforms that run open-weight models usually say so — it's a cost and privacy advantage worth leading with. Platforms that run frontier models on per-user licensing may not volunteer the architecture, but the pricing structure implies it.

**What counts as "done"?** Per-seat platforms often measure activity per user (drafts, queries, actions). Flat-rate platforms measure deliverables (the finished report, the published post, the triaged ticket). The work definition tells you whose problem the pricing solves: the platform's (seat utilization) or yours (output received).

## The two questions that settle the decision

The buying decision has two questions, not one. First: can the platform do the work? (That describes almost every capable AI tool in this market.) Second: does the pricing shape match how my team actually grows? This second question is the one that determines whether the tool stays or churns in month six.

Per-seat pricing works when you know exactly how many people will use the tool and none of them needs five functions each. Flat-rate, work-volume pricing works when your team shape is fluid — one person covering multiple functions, or a small team whose role coverage changes every sprint.

The architecture underneath — open-weight or frontier-licensed — is what makes the second model possible. Understanding that connection turns the pricing conversation from "which has the lower sticker price" into "which design matches how I actually work."

That's a comparison worth getting right before month three, when the bill arrives and you realize which model you're paying for.
