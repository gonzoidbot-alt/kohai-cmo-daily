# The Wednesday pipeline sweep: an operator's playbook

Deals rarely die out loud. They die quietly — a prospect who stopped replying, a proposal nobody sent, a follow-up that lived in your head instead of your calendar. The pipeline sweep is the weekly habit that catches them, and it is exactly the shape of work an AI colleague should carry while you sell.

This post is the playbook: what a sweep is, how to brief it once, and how it runs every Wednesday without you rebuilding anything.

## Why do deals stall in the first place?

Most stalls aren't decisions — they're gaps. A deal with no next step has no pulse, and nobody notices until the month closes and the number disappoints.

Founders and lean teams lose more revenue to silence than to competition. The fix isn't discipline; it's a standing appointment with your CRM that someone else keeps for you.

## What is a pipeline sweep?

A pipeline sweep is a scheduled pass over your pipeline that finds stalled deals and moves each one forward — a follow-up drafted, a nudge written, or a clean close-out logged. On Kohai it's a recurring brief on a schedule: "Wed 14:00, Pipeline sweep." Set once, runs weekly.

It's the same shape as the Monday growth report and the Friday invoice chase: fixed inputs, a fixed deadline, one sensitive step held for your approval. Recurring work with a clear definition of done is the work AI colleagues handle best.

## How do you brief it?

One message — the whole job description, the way you'd brief a capable hire:

> "Every Wednesday at 2pm, review my pipeline. Flag every deal with no activity in the last 10 days. For each one, read our last exchange and draft the right next touch — a follow-up if we're waiting on them, a specific nudge if we're waiting on me, or a polite close-out if it's gone cold. Queue the drafts for my review before anything sends, and update the deal stages to match."

No workflow diagram, no trigger configuration, no field mapping. You'll refine the criteria after the first run — a one-sentence re-brief, not a rebuild.

## What does your colleague do vs what you do?

The division of labor in a weekly sweep looks like this:

| Sweep stage | Your AI colleague does | You do |
|---|---|---|
| Detection | Scans the CRM weekly, flags deals with no recent activity | Set the stall threshold — 10 days, 14, whatever fits your cycle |
| Diagnosis | Reads the last thread and sorts each stall: theirs, yours, or dead | Spot-check the sorting for the first few weeks |
| Drafting | Writes the right touch per deal — follow-up, nudge, or close-out | Read the drafts; this is where your voice matters |
| Sending | Holds every send at the approval gate | Approve, edit, or kill |
| Records | Updates deal stages and logs the activity in the CRM | Overrule anything that looks wrong |

Two rows carry the quality. Diagnosis is the difference between a real sweep and a mail merge — the colleague reads what was actually said, so the nudge references the conversation instead of guessing at it. And sending never happens on its own, because a message going to a prospect under your name deserves your eyes first.

## Where does the approval gate sit?

On the sends and on the records that matter. The sweep drafts everything and queues it; nothing goes to a prospect and no stage you set gets overwritten until you approve.

That line is what makes the automation safe to run unattended. A colleague that can find fifteen stalled deals but cannot send fifteen emails without your go is a colleague you can leave running over a long weekend.

## What does the sweep connect to?

A sweep is only as good as what it can read — the CRM where deals live, the inbox where the last conversation happened, the calendar where the next step gets booked. On Kohai, "~1,000 integrations connected day one, with per-agent tool scoping."

Connected on day one means no wiring session before the first sweep runs. Per-agent scoping means the Sales & Growth Lead sees the CRM, the inbox, and the calendar — not your billing, your codebase, or anything you haven't scoped to it. The sweep needs three systems; it gets three systems.

## What does the whole week look like?

One sweep compounds with the rest of the schedule. Here is a week of recurring automations running as a set:

| Day | Recurring automation | What lands in your Library |
|---|---|---|
| Mon 9:00 | Growth report | Last week's numbers, what moved, one decision to make |
| Wed 14:00 | Pipeline sweep | Follow-ups, nudges, and close-outs queued for your approval |
| Fri 16:00 | Invoice chase — needs approval | Overdue-invoice nudges drafted and waiting |

Kohai's example week: 24 deliverables shipped, 31 hours reclaimed. The sweep is the Wednesday line item in that number — and the pattern generalizes: pick the recurring work with fixed inputs and one sensitive step, brief it once, and let the schedule hold the habit instead of you.

## What does running it cost?

Sweeping and drafting are text work — deliverables priced in the tens of credits each, not hundreds. A month of weekly sweeps fits inside an entry plan, and you can see what each deliverable costs before you brief it. The plan price caps the month before it starts; no usage meter decides how much pipeline hygiene you can afford.

## Start with one sweep

Set the threshold at ten days, schedule it for Wednesday at 2pm, and review the first batch the way you'd review a new hire's first week. The first batch tells you whether the diagnosis is right; if it isn't, re-briefing is one sentence.

By the third week your pipeline is never more than a week stale — a condition most small teams have never experienced. That's the whole adoption path: one sweep, one schedule, then the rest of the week.
