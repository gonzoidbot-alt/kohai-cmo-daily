# How to choose an AI workforce platform: six criteria that decide it

Short answer: score every vendor on the same six criteria — staffing, output, control, data handling, pricing shape, and integration — and let the scores, not the demos, make the decision. An AI workforce platform is a pre-staffed team of AI colleagues you brief like hires, and the vendors in this category look similar in a demo while differing enormously in the six places that matter after week one.

This post is the checklist. It's written so you can score any platform on it — including ours — in an afternoon.

## What is an AI workforce platform?

It's software where the unit you buy is a role, not a workflow. You describe an outcome — "find 50 e-commerce leads and draft the outreach" — and named colleagues (sales, marketing, ops, support) produce finished deliverables: documents, spreadsheets, decks, drafted emails.

The distinction matters because the market also sells the builder shape: platforms where you assemble workflows yourself. Both are legitimate. If you've already decided to build, this checklist isn't for you. If you want to hire, the six criteria below decide which hire is worth making.

## What are the six criteria?

| # | Criterion | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Staffing model | Do I brief roles, or assemble workflows? |
| 2 | Output shape | Do I get finished deliverables, or run logs? |
| 3 | Control | What stops before I approve it? |
| 4 | Data handling | What happens to my data, and my clients'? |
| 5 | Pricing shape | Does the bill scale with seats or with usage? |
| 6 | Integration | What can it reach on day one? |

Demo scripts are built around criteria 1 and 6, because that's where vendors look identical. The purchase is decided by 2 through 5, because that's where the week-three differences live.

## How do you score each criterion?

**1. Staffing.** Ask what exists before you sign up. On a workforce platform, colleagues arrive pre-staffed — you brief them, scope their tools, and redirect. If "setup" means mapping your task into nodes and triggers, you're evaluating a builder and should score it as one.

**2. Output.** Ask where the work lands. Finished deliverables in a Library you can open, review, and forward is the standard; a chat transcript you have to mine is a step backwards from the spreadsheet you were already using. One test: can the output of today's run be tomorrow's attachment without retyping?

**3. Control.** Ask what waits for your approval. Sensitive actions — emails sent, invoices touched, anything client-facing — should stop at a gate until you say go, and that gate should be yours to keep on permanently, not a setting the vendor nudges you to relax. A platform confident in its review loop will say "you stay in control" and mean it; one that sells full autonomy as the dream is selling you its support tickets.

**4. Data handling.** This is the criterion buyers skip until a client asks, so ask it first: which models run the work, and what are the providers contractually allowed to do with the data in transit? The verifiable answer sounds like this: **"Open-weight models only — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — from certified no-training providers."** Add isolation to the same question: is your work separated per tenant, and can each colleague's tool access be scoped to its role? If a vendor can't answer in two sentences, that answer is the score.

**5. Pricing shape.** Ignore the dollar figure for a moment — pricing shapes decide more than prices. Per-seat pricing means the bill grows every time a teammate touches the tool; per-plan pricing with unlimited seats doesn't. Credit metering is fine when the costs are published per task type and failed tasks are free. Run the math on your real week, not the vendor's example week.

**6. Integration.** Ask what's connected on day one and how tools are scoped. Breadth without scoping is a liability — a colleague that can read your books shouldn't also be able to email your clients unless you said so.

## What should you ask every vendor?

1. Show me a finished deliverable your platform produced — not a chat about one.
2. Which actions stop for approval, and can I keep them gated indefinitely?
3. Which models run my work, and do the providers train on it?
4. What does a bad run cost me — literally, in credits or dollars?
5. How many seats are included, and what does seat thirteen cost?
6. What can each colleague touch on day one, and who sets that scope?

A vendor that answers all six with specifics is scoreable. One that answers with roadmap is telling you what week three looks like.

## How do you test one in a week?

Score first from the vendor's site and docs — every answer above should be public. Then brief two real outcomes from different functions on day one: one sales task, one ops task. By day three you'll have data no demo provides: time to first finished deliverable, minutes you spent correcting, and whether the output was attachable. Pick the platform that scored well *before* the trial and confirmed it during it. If you want a baseline to score against, Kohai is free to start — no card, limited time — and plans begin at $20/month with unlimited seats on every plan. Run the checklist on us the same way you'd run it on anyone.
