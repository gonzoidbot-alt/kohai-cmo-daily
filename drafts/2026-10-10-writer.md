# How to compare AI platforms when your work touches client data

When your business handles client data — contracts, financials, health-adjacent notes — the comparison question changes. It stops being "which platform does the most" and becomes "which platform can I describe to my clients without hedging." One axis decides it: which models run the work, and what happens to your data on the way through.

Most comparison content skips that axis because it's uncomfortable. It narrows the field, and some good products don't survive it. This post runs the comparison through it anyway, because for a client-data business the other axes are negotiable and this one isn't.

## Why does the model layer decide this?

Because the platform you buy is not the model that does the work. Most AI products send your prompts and files to third-party model providers, and the platform's privacy policy — the one you read at signup — doesn't govern what those providers do. Your data's journey has two legs, and the second leg is the one almost nobody asks about.

That matters more when the data isn't only yours. A solo founder leaking their own drafts loses drafts; an accountant, a lawyer, or a fractional CFO leaking client material loses clients. The exposure scales with who else's information rides in the brief, which is why the evaluation standard should be evidence, not assurances.

## What must a vendor show, not say?

Four claims show up in every vendor's marketing, and each has an evidence version that separates an answer from a shrug:

| What vendors say | What to require instead | What a non-answer looks like |
|---|---|---|
| "We use the best models" | The model stack, named, in one sentence | If they can't name the models, they can't tell you who trains on your data either |
| "Your data is safe" | A no-training commitment at the provider level, in writing | "Safe" is a feeling; certification is a fact |
| "Enterprise-grade security" | Per-tenant isolation and encryption, described in the docs | A pricing tier name is not an architecture |
| "We don't train on your data" | Which entity commits — the platform or the model provider? | A platform policy can't bind a third-party provider it doesn't own |

The last row is the one that catches most buyers. The platform can promise anything about its own systems; the training decision happens at the model provider, one leg further down. Ask about that leg specifically, and notice which vendors have already answered it in their docs.

## How do the category shapes handle data?

The three shapes of AI platform answer the data question differently, and none of the answers is disqualifying — but each comes with its own question to ask:

| Shape | Where your data goes | The question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Workflow builders (n8n, Relevance AI, similar) | Through the model APIs *you* configure | Whose API keys are those, and what do those providers' terms say about training? |
| Frontier assistant platforms | To closed frontier models under the platform's terms | Does the platform's policy reach the model provider, or stop at the platform? |
| Pre-staffed AI teams (Kohai's shape) | To the platform's own published model stack | Can they state the whole arrangement in one sentence? |

The generous reading holds here, as it should in any honest comparison: a builder gives you control precisely because you hold the keys — and the responsibility that comes with them. A frontier platform gives you capability, and the due diligence is checking whether its commitments extend past its own edge. Neither is wrong for the buyer who wants it. The failure mode is only buying one while assuming a different shape's privacy posture.

## What can you tell your own clients?

This is the practical test the tables serve. When a client asks what happens to their files in your tools, the useful answer is a sentence you can send them — not a reassurance you have to assemble live.

Kohai's answer is on the record, and it's one sentence: **"Open-weight models only — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — from certified no-training providers."** Per-tenant isolation and encryption sit under that, so each workspace's material stays separated from every other tenant's. Open weights make the arrangement verifiable rather than promised: the models are published, the providers are named, and the commitment is about training, not vibes.

If a vendor on your shortlist can't produce its version of that sentence, that's a finding, not a rude question. The sentence exists either way — you just want to know whose it is before your client asks for it.

## What stays under your control regardless of the model layer?

The model layer isn't the whole privacy posture, and the rest is worth checking in the same pass. Per-agent tool scoping decides which colleagues can touch which tools — the one chasing invoices doesn't need your social accounts — and approval gates hold the steps with outside impact until you say go. Scope and gates live at the platform level, so they're checkable from a demo or a trial without a security questionnaire.

The full posture, then, has three layers: what the models do with your data, what the colleagues can reach, and what waits for your approval. A vendor who is quiet on one of the three is quiet for a reason worth finding out.

## How do you verify it before committing?

Use the trial as the evidence phase. The 7-day free trial puts a first deliverable in your Library the same day — brief one real task with real (non-client or anonymized) material, ask your shortlist the four evidence questions from the table above, and see which ones answer in a sentence.

The comparison this axis produces is shorter than most, because the axis is binary in practice: either a vendor can account for what its models do with your work, or it can't yet. For a business whose clients trust it with their records, that's not a tiebreaker. It's the first question.
