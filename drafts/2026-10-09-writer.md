# What is an AI workforce? A team with names, not software with settings

An AI workforce is a team of named AI colleagues with defined roles — a chief of staff, a sales lead, an operations lead — briefed the way you'd brief capable hires, doing the work in your existing tools, and delivering finished results into your Library. It is not one general-purpose assistant you manage task by task, and it is not software you configure until it behaves.

That distinction decides what the technology can do for your business. Software with settings does what you set up. A team with names does what you brief — and keeps doing it after you stop paying attention.

## Why do the names matter?

Because a name is where three practical abilities come from, and all three are missing from a blank chat box:

1. **Briefability.** A role has standing responsibilities. "Every Monday, the growth report — pipeline changes, support volume, cash position — one page" is a complete brief to an Operations Lead, because the role already carries the context of what a report is for.
2. **Correctability.** You correct a colleague in a sentence: "add cash position to next Monday's." The correction sticks, because it attaches to a role that has a memory of its work. You cannot correct a chat box — only re-prompt it, forever.
3. **Scopability.** A role's access matches its job. The colleague chasing invoices doesn't need your social accounts, and a team organized by role makes that boundary the default rather than a setting you hunt for.

None of this requires you to learn anything new. It's just how work with people already works — which is the point. The interface to an AI workforce should be the management skill you already have, not a configuration skill you'd have to acquire.

## What does the org chart look like?

On Kohai, the team arrives staffed. Mochi, your Chief of Staff, sits at the top — you brief Mochi once, in one message, and the goal is split into tasks and handed to the right specialist:

| Role | Owns | Example brief |
|---|---|---|
| Mochi — Chief of Staff | Inbox, calendar, meeting prep, notes, follow-ups | "Prep my Thursday investor meeting; brief me 15 minutes before" |
| Sales & Growth Lead | Prospects, sequences, replies | "Find 50 e-commerce leads and draft the outreach" |
| Marketing & Comms Lead | Posts, emails, content, landing copy | "Turn last week's notes into this week's post" |
| Operations Lead | Status reporting, automations, sprint planning | "Monday 9:00: the growth report, one page" |
| Customer Support Lead | Tickets, triaged around the clock | "Triage new tickets daily; draft replies, don't send" |

This is why "workforce" is a claim about structure, not vocabulary. Twelve chatbots with cute names is not a workforce if you still route every task yourself. A workforce is when you brief one colleague and the coordination happens without you.

## How is this different from an AI assistant?

An assistant is a good product — for tasks. The difference shows up in how the work gets handed over and who holds it:

| | AI assistant | AI workforce |
|---|---|---|
| Who you brief | Every task, from scratch | One colleague, once per role |
| Who coordinates | You | The chief of staff |
| Where output lives | A chat thread you mine | Deliverables in your Library |
| Sensitive steps | However the prompt lands | Waiting at an approval gate |
| Getting better | Your prompts improve | The role improves |

The last row is the compounding one. With an assistant, the skill lives in you — stop writing careful prompts and output quality decays. With a workforce, the skill lives in the role: the brief, the check, the correction you made in week one. That's the difference between renting effort and employing a hire.

## What does a staffed team actually produce?

Kohai's example week: 24 deliverables shipped, 31 hours reclaimed. The reclaimed hours split the way the roles would predict — sales outreach 11, content & SEO 8, ops & reporting 7, support triage 5. Each workstream maps to a named colleague, which is what makes the number auditable rather than impressive: you can look at any deliverable and say who owns the work behind it.

That auditability is the quiet test for the whole category. Ask any "workforce" vendor: when work ships, is there a role it belongs to — someone you'd correct, rescope, or praise? If the answer is a chat log, you have a tool with a team costume on.

## What stays yours?

The gate. In a staffed week, most work runs ahead — drafts, research, reports, deliverables assembled for your Library. The steps with outside impact wait: sends, spends, deletions. You approve the send, not the draft. It's the same latitude you'd give a trusted hire, with the same conversation before anything irreversible.

## How do you hire one?

You don't assemble it. You brief it — one message describing the outcome, the way you'd write a short job description, and the team assembles itself around it. The 7-day free trial is long enough to brief one recurring task, read what lands in your Library on day one, and see whether managing roles feels more like managing tools or like managing people.

If it's the latter, you've found the thing the category name promised. And you'll know it by the only measure that matters: work coming back finished, from colleagues you can name.
