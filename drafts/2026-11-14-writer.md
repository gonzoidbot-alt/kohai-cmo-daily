# Your Pipeline Doesn't Need an SDR. It Needs Someone Who Never Forgets a Follow-Up.

If your outreach keeps stalling after the first send, the fix isn't more prospecting — it's handing follow-up to a colleague who never lets a thread go quiet.

That's the pattern most small teams live with. Someone built a lead list. Someone drafted a sequence. The first touch went out — and then reality happened. A launch, a support spike, a client fire. The second touch slips to next week, next week becomes never, and the pipeline turns into a graveyard of half-started conversations. Nobody failed on purpose. The work that follows the first send is simply the kind that gets displaced, because it's repetitive, low-drama, and always looks less urgent than whatever is on fire.

This is a playbook for that specific problem. It's the second half of the lead-list-to-outreach workflow — what happens after the first touch — and it's the half an AI colleague is built for.

## Why follow-up dies in small teams

The honest answer: follow-up has no natural owner. The founder who sent the first email is now in a sales call. The GTM lead who queued the sequence is now in a planning meeting. Every touch that isn't the first one requires remembering, and remembering is the first casualty of a full calendar.

There's also a bookkeeping problem hiding inside it. Follow-up depends on knowing where every conversation stands: who was contacted, when, what they said, what the next touch should reference. When that state lives in someone's head or in a spreadsheet last opened two weeks ago, every follow-up starts with ten minutes of archaeology. Ten minutes per prospect is usually more than the touch itself is worth, so the touch doesn't happen.

A colleague doesn't have a calendar conflict and doesn't need archaeology. The state lives in the workspace, and the work runs on schedule.

## The brief is one message

You brief it the way you'd brief a capable hire. One message, outcome first:

> "Here's our list of 40 open prospects from last month. For each one: check the thread history, note where it stopped, and draft the next touch — referencing whatever they last said or did. Three-touch sequence, plain and human, no templates. Queue every send for my approval. And build me a tracker that shows status for every prospect at a glance."

Notice what the brief does *not* contain: triggers, branching logic, field mappings. You described the outcome. The Sales & Growth Lead picks it up with the tools already connected — on Kōhai, ~1,000 integrations are connected day one, so the CRM, the inbox, and the history are reachable without you wiring anything.

Your job in this playbook is one thing only: define what a good next touch looks like. That's the judgment call a colleague can't make for you. Everything after it — the checking, the drafting, the scheduling, the logging — isn't.

## What lands in your Library

The first deliverable shows up the same day, and it looks like this:

- **A status line per prospect** — contacted, replied, gone quiet, closed, with the date of the last touch
- **Draft second and third touches** for every prospect whose thread stopped, each referencing the actual last exchange, not a generic "just bumping this"
- **A send queue** — nothing goes out until you've looked at it
- **A tracker** — one view of the whole pipeline, sorted by who's gone quiet longest

You review the drafts the way you'd review a new hire's first batch of emails: quickly, correcting tone once, and less each week after.

## The tracker is real software, not a spreadsheet

The tracker deserves its own section, because it's the part most teams skip — and skipping it is why follow-up keeps dying.

The usual version of a "pipeline tracker" is a shared sheet. It's accurate the day someone builds it and wrong within a week, because updating it is exactly the kind of displaced work we started with. A tracker that depends on someone remembering to maintain it will always drift.

On Kōhai, you can ask for something better in the same brief. **App Studio ships deployed software, not mockups** — so the tracker you ask for is a real, hosted tool your colleague keeps current as part of doing the work, not a template someone has to remember to fill in. When a touch sends, the tracker knows. When a reply lands, the status changes. The record and the work are the same system.

For a lean team, that's the difference between a pipeline you can trust in a Monday meeting and a pipeline you have to reconstruct from inboxes every time someone asks a question.

## Every send waits at your gate

This is the part worth being plain about, because outreach is where "set it and forget it" goes wrong.

Nothing in this playbook sends itself. Every drafted touch sits in a queue at an approval gate until you say go. You can approve a batch in two minutes on Monday morning, edit one draft before it ships, or kill a thread where the context has changed. You stay in control — not as a slogan, but as the actual mechanism, because the sends physically can't happen without you.

That gate is also what makes the system safe to hand more work over time. Once you trust the drafts, you can approve in batches. The colleague gets faster; the judgment stays yours.
