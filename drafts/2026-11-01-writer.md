# How many AI tools does your business actually need?

However many you're paying for right now, the honest answer is "fewer." Most small businesses running on AI have assembled their stack one subscription at a time — a writing assistant here, a research tool there, something for transcription, something for social scheduling — and nobody has ever added the column up. The stack works. It also quietly costs more than hiring an actual team, and it comes with a ceiling on every tool you pay for.

This post is the audit most operators never run: what the stack really costs, why it grows, and what one predictable plan replaces.

## What is your AI stack actually costing you?

Add it up in three columns: the subscription, the seat, and the ceiling. A typical solo operator's stack looks like this:

| Tool category | Typical monthly cost | What the cap buys you |
|---|---|---|
| Writing assistant | $20–30 | One or two seats, word limits |
| Research/deep-research tool | $20–40 | A handful of deep searches per month |
| Meeting transcription | $15–25 | Per-seat fee, limited hours |
| Social scheduling | $15–30 | Per-seat or per-channel |
| Workflow/automation builder | $20–50 | Task or step quotas |
| **Total** | **$90–175/mo** | **Every line has a cap** |

That's $1,100–2,100 a year for software that still needs you to operate each piece: open the right tool, write the right prompt, copy the output to the next tool, and remember which subscription has quota left this week. The caps matter as much as the cost — the moment you actually need the tool, you find its limit.

And if anyone else on the team touches these tools, the per-seat pricing multiplies. The stack that costs $100 for you costs $300 for a three-person team.

## Why does the stack keep growing?

Because each AI tool is built to do one job, and your business doesn't have one job. Monday is ops, Tuesday is outreach, Wednesday is content, and each day sends you to a different subscription with a different interface, a different billing model, and a different failure mode.

The pile grows the same way browser tabs do: every new task feels like it needs a new tool, because the tools are sold as point solutions. Six months later you're a small-business AI stack manager, which was never the job you hired yourself for.

There's a maintenance cost too, beyond the invoices. Point-solution AI means you own the glue between the tools — the copy-paste plumbing, the exports, the "did anyone check what the research tool produced before it went in the deck?" That glue is your unpaid second job, and it doesn't show up on any invoice.

## What would one predictable plan replace?

The alternative isn't a bigger stack. It's a team: named AI colleagues covering the functions you're currently buying separate tools for — a Marketing & Comms Lead for posts and content, a Sales & Growth Lead for prospects and sequences, an Operations Lead for reporting and automation, a Customer Support Lead for tickets — all working inside one plan with one billing model.

That's the case for bundling, and the pricing has to make the bundle actually cheaper than the pile. Here's the current math, verified on kohai.co/pricing: "Kōhai Pro is $100/month for 40,000 credits with unlimited team seats on every plan." Unlimited seats is the part worth pausing on — every per-seat line in your current stack collapses to zero, whether it's you or three teammates briefing colleagues.

The entry numbers are equally direct: Kōhai Free is $0, no card, for a limited time (first 1,000 users keep it), and paid plans run $20, $100, and $200 a month. One invoice. No seat multiplication.

## Why can a bundled plan be this cheap?

Because of what the platform runs on. Kohai runs exclusively on open-weight models — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — from inference providers certified not to train on your data, with per-tenant isolation and encryption. That's a privacy stance, but it's also the economics: the platform isn't reselling a frontier API's premium at a markup. The savings of open-weight models get passed into the credit price instead of absorbed into your bill.

The credit model then makes the bill legible before you spend it. A small task (an email draft, a list edit) runs 5–10 credits. A medium task — a chart, a landing page, a blog post — runs 20–50. A large task (a deck, deep research) runs 100–300. On the Pro plan's 40,000 monthly credits, that's hundreds of deliverables, priced before you assign them.

Two details make the predictability real rather than marketing:

1. **Failed tasks consume 0 credits.** A task that doesn't deliver costs nothing. You never pay for a retry loop.
2. **Add-on credits never expire.** A heavy month doesn't punish you twice.

Compare that to a per-seat subscription with a hard usage ceiling, where hitting the limit mid-project means either upgrading the whole plan or stopping work.

## How do you run the swap in one afternoon?

1. **List every AI subscription you pay for** — including the ones billed annually that you forgot about. Write the monthly-equivalent cost next to each.
2. **Mark which ones are per-seat.** Multiply by heads. This is usually the moment the total stops feeling abstract.
3. **Circle the work, not the tools.** Under each subscription, write the recurring job it does: "weekly report," "outreach drafts," "support replies." Jobs, not features.
4. **Brief a colleague for each circled job.** One message is the whole job description — the same brief you'd give a new hire on day one. The first deliverable lands in your Library the same day.
5. **Keep the gate on.** Sensitive actions wait at an approval gate until you say go, and each colleague only reaches the tools you scoped for it — per-agent tool scoping out of ~1,000 integrations connected day one. You stay in control of exactly the parts you'd want to.

Then cancel what the colleagues absorbed. Most stacks shrink by more than half; the honest test is your own invoice line, not this table.

## The question behind the question

"How many AI tools do I need?" is really "how much of my team's work can run on one predictable bill?" Point-solution pricing sells you capabilities. Workforce pricing sells you outcomes: named colleagues, briefed like hires, shipping deliverables into a Library you review — with the bill known before the week starts.

If the column you added up in the first table came in over $100, the math is already on the table. Start free, run one week of real work through a team instead of a stack, and let the next invoice make the argument.
