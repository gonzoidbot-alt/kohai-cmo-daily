# How to delegate ops work to AI: the brief that gets it done

Most attempts to delegate operations work to AI fail the same way: the founder writes a prompt, gets back something almost right, and spends the evening fixing it. The problem isn't the tool. It's that a prompt is an instruction for a machine, when what the work needs is a brief for a colleague.

The difference is practical, not philosophical. A brief states the outcome, the audience, the constraints, and the check — and then gets out of the way. Write one well and the deliverable arrives finished. Write it as a prompt and you've bought yourself a first draft.

## What does delegating ops to AI actually look like?

One message. You describe what done looks like — "the Monday report pulls pipeline changes, support volume, and cash position into one page I can read in five minutes" — and a colleague picks it up from there. The work happens in the tools you already use, and the finished deliverable lands in your Library.

There's no workflow to diagram and no prompt to iterate on. If your first instinct is to write step-by-step instructions, you're doing the colleague's job for it — and doing it worse, because you don't know how the tools connect and it does.

## Why do most AI delegation attempts fail?

Three reasons, and all three live in the brief:

1. **Instructions instead of outcomes.** "Write a follow-up email to the Q3 leads mentioning the webinar" produces one email. "Chase every Q3 lead that went quiet after the webinar and tell me who's warm" produces a pipeline result.
2. **No definition of done.** If you don't say what finished looks like — length, audience, format, where it lands — you'll judge the output against a picture in your head the colleague never saw.
3. **No check.** Delegation without a verification step either gets over-trusted or under-trusted. Both are bad. One is dangerous; the other is wasted.

The fix is the same for all three, and it fits in a template.

## What goes in the brief?

Five parts. Fill them in for any recurring task and the quality gap between draft one and draft three mostly disappears:

| Part | What it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome | What exists when this is done? | "A one-page Monday report I can read in five minutes" |
| Audience | Who reads or receives it? | "Me — plain language, no vendor jargon" |
| Sources | Where does the truth live? | "HubSpot pipeline, support inbox, Stripe balance" |
| Constraints | What must it never do? | "Never email anyone — draft only, I send" |
| Check | How do we both know it's right? | "Flag any number you couldn't verify" |

The constraints row deserves emphasis. This is where you decide, per task, what runs ahead and what waits for you — and it's why delegation doesn't require trusting the tool with everything. Your Sales & Growth Lead can touch the CRM and stay out of your accounting. That scoping is per agent, not global.

## How much should you hand over at once?

One recurring task. Not five, not the whole ops layer — one task you'd otherwise do this week anyway, briefed with the template above.

The first-week sequence that works: brief the task on Monday, review the deliverable Tuesday against your definition of done, correct it in one sentence if it missed, and let the corrected brief stand as the new standing instruction. By week two you're reviewing instead of writing. That's the actual leverage point of delegating ops to AI — not the first deliverable, but never writing that brief again.

Founders who hand over five tasks at once get the opposite result: five half-reviewed outputs, no corrected briefs, and a conclusion that "AI needs too much management." The management was in the onboarding, not the tool.

## What happens to your data when you delegate?

This is the question that stops the handover, and it deserves a direct answer rather than a shrug. A brief hands a colleague your pipeline, your inbox, your client names — so the model layer underneath matters as much as the workflow on top.

Kohai's answer is structural: **open-weight models only — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — from certified no-training providers.** Your work isn't training anyone's model, and it stays isolated per tenant. Delegation shouldn't cost you ownership of the work you delegated, and on this stack it doesn't.

## What does the first delegated week look like?

Concretely: Monday, one brief, using the five-part template. Wednesday, the deliverable arrives in your Library; you check it against the outcome line, not line by line. Friday, you either correct the brief once and let it run standing, or you hand over the next task.

Work in your tools — around ~1,000 integrations connected day one, with per-agent tool scoping — means the colleague shows up already wired into the sources your brief names. There's no integration project between you and the first deliverable.

That's the whole method: brief the outcome, scope the constraints, hand over one task, review against done. The founders who delegate ops work well aren't better at AI. They're better at writing the brief — and now you have the template.
