# Stop writing prompts. Start briefing a colleague.

Somewhere along the way, using AI became a skill you were expected to study. Courses, prompt libraries, chain-of-thought recipes — an entire discipline built around asking a machine for things in exactly the right words.

It's worth asking what that skill is for. When you hand work to a capable person, you don't compose an incantation. You describe the outcome, answer their questions once, and let them run. AI work can work the same way — and for a founder whose week is already full, it should.

## What's the difference between a prompt and a brief?

A prompt is a single instruction for a single task: write this email, summarize this thread, draft that post. It does its job, and then it's gone — along with everything you explained to make it work.

A brief is a standing description of a role and its outcome: "Every Friday, chase overdue invoices politely and hold the sends for my approval." You give it once. It keeps working.

Prompting is per-task labor. Briefing is a hire.

## Why is prompting a tax on small teams?

Three costs stack up. The first is re-explaining: every prompt starts from zero, so you re-describe your business, your customers, and your tone every single time. The second is variance: the same prompt gives you a different answer on Tuesday than it did on Monday, so you review everything like it's new. The third is the skill itself — prompt engineering is a craft, and craft takes hours you don't have.

For a solo operator, those costs land in the only currency that's already overspent: your attention. The tools work. The asking is still your job.

| | Prompt-first | Task-first — brief a colleague |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | Every task, from zero | Once, per role |
| Context | Re-explained each time | The colleague keeps it |
| Skill required | Prompt writing | Managing — which you already have |
| Sensitive steps | Whatever you remember to check | Held at an approval gate by default |
| Output | A chat reply to copy out | A deliverable in your Library |

## What does a brief actually look like?

One message — the way you'd brief a capable hire on their first morning:

> "You're my Operations Lead. Every Friday at 4pm, check Stripe for overdue invoices, draft a friendly nudge for each one, and queue the drafts for my approval before anything sends. These are relationships, not debts — keep the tone warm."

No system prompt to maintain, no examples to paste in, no settings to tune. One message is the whole job description, because the role — not the wording — carries the context.

If the first run misses, you correct it the way you'd correct a person: "shorter," "skip anything under $50," "CC me on the big accounts." A sentence, not a rewrite.

## Doesn't good prompting still matter?

Honestly, at the margins, yes. A precise ask gets a sharper reply, and if you enjoy that craft, prompt-first tools are built for you — that's a legitimate way to work, and the people who are good at it get good results.

But the gap between a decent prompt and a great one is a better reply. The gap between prompting at all and briefing a colleague is your evenings. A founder optimizing prompts is optimizing the wrong layer — the questions, not the outcomes.

## Who's actually doing the work?

The roles matter more than the models. Mochi is your Chief of Staff; a Sales & Growth Lead and an Operations Lead take their own lanes. You brief the role, and the colleague works inside it.

And the brief can be short because the wiring is already done. On Kohai, "~1,000 integrations connected day one, with per-agent tool scoping" — your Operations Lead can reach Stripe and the inbox without reaching your codebase or your accounting. You don't spend a sentence of the brief explaining which tools to use; you spend it on what done looks like.

## What does a briefed week produce?

Once the prompts become roles, the work stops being a series of asks and becomes a schedule. The Monday report assembles itself. The invoice chase runs on Friday. The meeting briefs arrive before the meetings.

"Kohai's example week: 24 deliverables shipped, 31 hours reclaimed." Those aren't 24 chat replies — they're reports, drafts, summaries, and follow-ups you would otherwise have produced yourself, one prompt at a time. Thirty-one hours is roughly four working days a month that stop being spent asking.

## Try one brief instead of ten prompts

Take the task you prompted most this week — the report, the follow-ups, the meeting prep — and brief it as a standing role instead: the outcome, the schedule, the decisions that stay yours. The 7-day free trial covers the whole experiment.

If the brief holds, you've stopped paying the per-task tax, and the skill you never needed to learn stays unlearned. If it misses, re-briefing is one sentence — which is still faster than writing a perfect prompt.
