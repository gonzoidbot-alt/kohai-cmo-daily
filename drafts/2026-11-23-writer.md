# One AI Assistant or a Whole Team? What a Solo Founder Actually Needs

A solo founder needs a team, not an assistant — because your week isn't one job, it's five. An assistant extends whatever workflow you're standing in at the moment. A team covers the week: the metrics, the pipeline, the content, the tickets, the status report.

That distinction sounds like marketing hair-splitting, so let's make it concrete. If you've been working with a general-purpose AI assistant and still feel like nothing is off your plate, the problem usually isn't the model. It's the shape of the arrangement.

## What does an AI assistant actually cover?

An assistant is a generalist you prompt, task by task. You bring the context, it returns the draft. Used well, it's genuinely good at that: summarizing a long thread, tightening a paragraph, turning notes into an outline.

The limits show up around the edges of each task. The assistant doesn't know what happened in your pipeline last week unless you paste it in. It doesn't check the support inbox on Saturday. It doesn't remember that the invoice from the October client is still unpaid — you remember, and you prompt, and you move the output to wherever the work actually lives. The model is capable; you are the integration layer between your tools and your tasks, and you were already the bottleneck. That's why the week still feels full.

There's a second, quieter cost: every task starts from zero. The prompt you wrote for last Monday's report doesn't run again on its own. An assistant has no standing, no schedule, no seat in your tools.

## What changes when the hires are a team?

You brief a colleague the way you'd brief a new hire: one message that describes the outcome, not the keystrokes. "Find 50 e-commerce leads and draft the outreach" is a complete job description. The finished work lands in your Library — documents, spreadsheets, decks, even deployed sites.

The team part matters because your week divides into functions, and functions can be owned. Kohai ships them as named roles: a Strategy Lead for metrics and dashboards, a Sales & Growth Lead for prospects and sequences, a Marketing & Comms Lead for posts and emails, a Customer Support Lead for triage around the clock, an Operations Lead for reporting and planning. Dozens of templates cover the rest — research, finance, legal, recruiting — or you define a custom role.

Here's the week that shape produces, function by function:

| Function | What a generalist assistant does | What a named colleague does |
|---|---|---|
| Metrics & reporting | You export, paste, and prompt each time | Strategy Lead assembles the Monday report on a schedule |
| Sales outreach | Drafts a message when you ask | Sales & Growth Lead works the list, drafts the sequence, waits on sends |
| Content | Drafts one post per prompt | Marketing & Comms Lead drafts against the calendar you set once |
| Support | Answers when you paste a ticket | Support Lead triages the queue continuously |
| Ops & status | Helps when you bring the data | Operations Lead compiles status and flags what's off-track |

None of that requires you to build anything. There's no workflow editor, no node graph, no trigger configuration to maintain. If you *enjoy* designing automations, builder platforms are the right tool — that's a real discipline with real payoff. This post is for the other decision: you want the work done, not a system to maintain.

## Isn't a team five times the price?

No — and this is where the team framing usually surprises people. Seats aren't the meter; work is. Kohai charges credits per task, and every plan includes unlimited team seats, so adding a fourth or fifth colleague doesn't change your subscription.

The published rates are concrete. Medium work — a chart, a landing page, a blog post — runs 20–50 credits. Large work — a deck, a research brief — runs 100–300. Failed tasks consume zero credits, so the meter only moves when work lands. Plans are $20/mo for 8,000 credits, $100/mo Pro for 40,000 (the popular tier), and $200/mo Max for 80,000. A founder delegating a handful of recurring tasks sits comfortably on the entry plan.

## Do you lose control when colleagues act on their own?

You keep the decisions that matter. Sensitive actions — sending the outreach, spending money, publishing — wait at an approval gate until you say go. Your colleagues draft and stage; you approve and ship. And the scoping is per-agent: each colleague touches only the tools you've connected for its role, so the Support Lead isn't rifling through your finance folder.

This is the part worth being honest about: full autonomy is not the dream. A teammate that acts without checkable judgment is a liability wearing a lanyard. The gate is what makes delegation trustable enough to actually hand things over — you can start with low-risk tasks and widen the lane as the track record builds.

## What should you hand over first?

Start with the task that meets three conditions: you've done it the same way at least three times, its context already lives in a tool your colleagues can read, and a mistake would cost you an email rather than a client. Weekly report assembly, meeting-prep briefs, inbox triage, first-pass outreach drafts — these clear the bar easily. Keep pricing decisions, client relationships, and anything where taste *is* the product.

The compounding is the point. The site's own example week — 24 deliverables shipped, 31 hours reclaimed — isn't one heroic delegation. It's five modest ones, running every week, that you stopped doing by hand. Each task you hand off keeps its shape after you stop feeding it, which is precisely what a prompt in a chat window never does.

## Start smaller than you think

You don't need to staff five functions before this is worth doing. Pick the one recurring task that ate your Friday, brief it like you'd brief a competent hire, and read the first deliverable before dinner. If it's good, hand the task over permanently. If it needs direction, give it — that's a normal part of onboarding any teammate, human or otherwise.
