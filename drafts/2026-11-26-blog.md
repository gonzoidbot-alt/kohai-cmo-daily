# AI Customer Support for Small Business: The After-Hours Playbook

AI customer support for a small business works when you split the job in two: the routine tickets get resolved without you, and everything sensitive waits at an approval gate until morning. The payoff isn't a chatbot on your homepage — it's a queue that gets worked at 9 p.m. and on Sundays, so you start Monday at zero instead of forty-seven.

Here's the playbook for setting that up once and letting it run all week.

## Why support punishes small teams more than any other workload

Sales pauses when you stop. Content pauses when you stop. Support doesn't pause — it accumulates. Tickets arrive while you're in a client call, while you're asleep, while you're doing the work you actually bill for. And the cruel part is the mix: most of what's sitting in the queue doesn't need you. "Where's my order?" is a lookup. "How do I export my data?" is a doc link. Maybe one ticket in ten needs actual judgment — an angry customer, an edge case, a refund.

Estimate your own ratio before you believe ours: pull last week's tickets and count how many a competent hire could have answered without asking you. For most small teams it's the clear majority. That majority is the job to delegate. The minority is the job to keep.

## What the coverage actually looks like

The design question isn't "can AI answer support?" It's "which tickets resolve alone, which wait for your approval, and which come find you?" That's the escalation ladder, and you write it once, in plain language:

| Tier | What it covers | Who acts | You see it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 — Resolve | Order status, how-to questions, doc links, password resets | Support Lead answers from your docs and policies | In the daily log only |
| 2 — Draft | Refunds, discounts, billing disputes, anything money-adjacent | Support Lead drafts the reply and the action | At the approval gate, batched |
| 3 — Escalate | Angry customers, legal or safety language, VIP accounts, anything the docs don't cover | Nobody — it stops and finds you | Immediately, with a summary |

Tier 1 is where the hours go, and it's the tier people are afraid to hand over. The safeguard isn't trust — it's scope. Your Support Lead works the ticket queue and your help docs. It doesn't touch your accounting, and it can't post anywhere public. On Kohai, every colleague gets per-agent tool scoping, so the support teammate's access is the support job — nothing wider.

Tier 2 is where you stay genuinely in control. A refund is a promise with a dollar amount attached, and that decision stays yours. The teammate prepares everything — the draft reply, the suggested amount, the ticket context — and holds it at an approval gate until you say go. You approve a batch over coffee instead of composing replies at midnight.

Tier 3 is deliberately small. If the ladder is written well, only a handful of tickets a week reach it, and each one arrives with a summary of what's already known. You're doing judgment, not archaeology.

## The brief is one message

You don't assemble this. You describe it, the way you'd brief a new hire on their first day:

> "You handle support for [business]. Work the shared inbox and the ticket queue around the clock. Resolve anything covered by our docs — refund policy is 30 days, shipping FAQ is attached. For refunds over $50, discounts, or anything money-adjacent, draft the reply and hold it for my approval. If a customer is angry, mentions legal action, or the docs don't cover it, flag me immediately with a summary. Log everything you resolved in a daily digest I read at 9 a.m."

That's the whole job description. The Support Lead picks it up with the tools it needs already connected — Kohai connects to about a thousand integrations on day one, so your inbox, help docs, and ticketing tool are linked before you write the second sentence of the brief. The first deliverable — the digest, or the first batch of drafted replies — lands in your Library the same day.

Two refinements worth making in week one. First, tune Tier 1 aggressively: if a resolved answer reads wrong, add the correction to the docs rather than pulling the ticket back — the teammate gets better because its source of truth does. Second, keep the gate on sends longer than feels necessary. You can relax a gate once you trust the drafts; you can't un-send an email.

## Your first week, in five steps

1. **Day 1:** Export last month's tickets. Sort them into the three tiers yourself — this half-hour is the whole configuration.
2. **Day 1:** Write the brief using the template above, with your own policies and dollar thresholds attached.
3. **Day 2:** Let Tier 1 run on live tickets. Read every answer in the daily log. Correct the docs, not the teammate.
4. **Day 3–5:** Approve Tier 2 batches each morning. Note which drafts you'd have written differently — that list becomes next week's policy edits.
5. **Day 7:** Check the numbers: tickets resolved alone, gate approvals, escalations. If escalations are above one in ten, the ladder needs a tier, not more hours from you.

The end state is boring in the best way: the routine queue clears itself overnight, the money decisions wait for you, and the only tickets that reach your phone are the ones that actually needed a human. You didn't hire a night shift. You wrote one brief.
