# What is an AI employee actually worth to you?

Worth is arithmetic, not adjectives: an AI employee is worth it when the value of the hours it frees exceeds what the work costs in credits. Both sides of that comparison are knowable before you commit — one from your own calendar, one from Kohai's published per-task pricing.

The reason founders get stuck on this question is that they try to answer it in the abstract. You don't need a study; you need three numbers about your own week.

## What is an hour of your time actually worth?

Three rates, in rising order of honesty:

| Pricing method | How it works | Typical range |
|---|---|---|
| Replacement cost | What you'd pay someone to do the task | $20–50/hour |
| Market rate | What your skills bill at elsewhere | $75–200/hour |
| Revenue rate | What an hour of sales or product work returns | often 10x market |

The replacement rate is the floor — it's what delegating saves. The revenue rate is the ceiling — it's what the same hour produces when pointed at growth instead of admin. Most founders run their week closer to the floor than they'd like to admit, which is exactly the gap an AI colleague closes.

A quick way to see the gap: write down the five tasks that ate your worst hour last week. For most solo operators the list reads the same — the Monday report, the invoice chase, the inbox sweep, the meeting notes, the follow-ups that didn't send themselves. Then price each hour at replacement rate and add it up. A founder clearing four such hours a week is looking at $4,000–8,000 a year at the *floor* rate. That's before the ceiling rate enters the picture.

The trap is pricing your hour at what you'd pay for help and stopping there. The help-that-costs-$25 also takes four hours to do what took you one, needs managing, and doesn't touch the revenue rate at all. The right question isn't "what would a person cost" but "what is the hour worth in both directions" — what it costs when spent on admin, and what it returns when spent on sales.

## Which hours can actually be freed?

Not all of them, and the post pretends otherwise at its peril. The hours that transfer are the recurring, well-defined ones: reports, chases, sweeps, summaries, follow-ups. The hours that don't are judgment calls — pricing decisions, hiring, the sensitive client conversation.

That's also why connection matters here. Kohai's answer is on the record: "~1,000 integrations connected day one, with per-agent tool scoping." The colleague works inside the tools where your hours currently go, scoped to what each role needs — day-one connection is what turns reclaimed hours from a vendor claim into a live number. No hour is reclaimed by a tool that can't reach the work.

## Where is the break-even?

Run the arithmetic on Kohai's published example week — 24 deliverables shipped, 31 hours reclaimed:

| Your priced hour | Value of 31 reclaimed hours | Read |
|---|---|---|
| $25 (replacement) | $775/week | Clears a month of credits in days |
| $100 (market) | $3,100/week | Not a comparison — a rout |
| Revenue rate | compounding | The admin week was never the bottleneck week |

Even at the floor rate, one reclaimed week pays for the colleagues many times over — and the published example week is a busy week, not a hero week.

## What does this arithmetic get wrong?

Three honest corrections, because a break-even that only works in the brochure isn't a break-even.

First, freed hours aren't freed one-for-one. You spend minutes briefing and minutes at the approval gate — that's the design, not a defect. Budget a ramp: the first week nets maybe half the hours, the second week more, because the brief improves and the recurring tasks stop needing supervision.

Second, reclaimed hours only count if you point them somewhere. Thirty-one hours that fill with more admin return exactly zero. The arithmetic holds when the freed time has a destination — sales, product, the client work only you can do — and the honest way to ensure one is to name it before the trial starts.

Third, not every task transfers cleanly. A task with judgment baked in costs approval-gate time every run, and some weeks that erodes half the gain. The fix is sorting tasks by definition quality: the well-specified ones run hands-off; the judgment-heavy ones are worth delegating only when your hour is priced high enough to pay for the reviewing.

## How do you find *your* number?

Price one recurring task, brief it on the 7-day free trial, and time what you didn't do. Your break-even is your priced hour times the hours the trial gives back, against the credits the work consumed — three numbers, all visible before the trial ends.

If the number clears, you have the answer in your own figures. If it doesn't, you learned it for the cost of a week — which is cheaper than most ways of learning it.
