# What do you trust an AI chief of staff with?

An AI chief of staff works from the most exposed material your business has: your inbox, your calendar, and the content of every meeting on them. That breadth is the point of the role — and it's also why "is it safe?" is the question that actually decides the hire.

This post answers it in four parts: what the role can reach, what the models behind it do with what they read, which actions wait for your approval, and how you verify all of it before you commit.

## Why does the chief of staff need more trust than any other hire?

Because its job is context. A sales lead works from a lead list. A support lead sees tickets. The chief of staff sees the connective tissue between everything — who you met, what you promised, what's overdue, what you're worried about — because coordinating your week is impossible without it.

That makes it the role with the widest read access and the most to protect. It also makes the trust question unavoidable: you cannot get the value of the role by narrowing what it sees. You get it by being precise about what happens to what it sees.

## What does the access actually look like?

Concrete is better than abstract here. On Kohai, Mochi, your Chief of Staff, works from four kinds of material:

| Surface | What it holds | What the colleague does with it |
|---|---|---|
| Calendar | Commitments, attendees, meeting history | Preps each meeting; briefs you 15 minutes before |
| Inbox | Threads with clients, investors, vendors | Tracks open commitments; drafts follow-ups |
| Meetings | What was said, decisions, dates | Takes live notes; writes the summary for your Library |
| Connected tools | CRM, docs, project records | Keeps records current; hands context to the specialist leads |

Every row is material you already protect by habit — you don't paste client terms into a random web form, and you wouldn't forward a board thread to a stranger. The role is only safe if the same discipline exists underneath it.

## What keeps what it reads private?

**Open-weight models only — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — from certified no-training providers.** That sentence is the foundation, and it's checkable rather than promised: the models are publicly named, and the providers running them are certified not to train on your data. What Mochi reads in your inbox and hears in your meetings doesn't become anyone's training material.

Two more layers sit under that. Per-tenant isolation and encryption keep your workspace's material separated from every other tenant's — your calendar and client threads live in your tenancy, not in a shared pool. And the whole arrangement is stateable in one sentence, which matters more than it sounds: a privacy posture you can't summarize is a posture you can't verify.

## Where does the gate sit?

Access to read is not access to act. The meeting flow shows where the line falls, because it's the workflow most people picture first:

1. **Preps the meeting** — reads attendees, context, and your past threads, and drafts what you need to walk in knowing.
2. **Briefs you 15 minutes before** — who they are, what's open, what you committed to last time.
3. **Takes live notes** — so you can listen instead of typing.
4. **Writes the summary** — decisions, owners, dates, filed in your Library.
5. **Starts the follow-ups** — drafted and queued for review.

The first four steps are read-and-write work inside your workspace. The fifth is where the gate sits: anything with outside impact — an email to a client, a commitment made on your behalf — waits until you say go. You approve the send, not the draft.

## How is access scoped across the team?

The chief of staff has the widest surface because the role needs it; nobody else should. Per-agent tool scoping sets each colleague's reach to match its job — Mochi touches calendar and inbox, the colleague chasing invoices touches your billing tools, and neither sees what its role doesn't require.

That's the same boundary you'd draw for a human team, applied by default instead of by policy document. The trust question shrinks from "what can the AI see?" to "what does *this* role need?" — a question with a short, checkable answer per colleague.

## How do you verify this before you hire?

Ask for the sentence. Any vendor asking for your inbox and calendar should be able to state its model layer, its training commitments, and its isolation architecture in plain language — if the answer takes a policy document, that's a finding.

Then use the 7-day free trial as the verification, not the demo: brief one real meeting, watch the brief arrive 15 minutes before, read the summary, and look at the follow-up queue. You'll see exactly which steps ran on their own and which one waited for you.

The chief of staff is the highest-trust hire in an AI workforce, and it should be held to the standard a human in the role would get: named accountability, a defined scope, and nothing irreversible without your sign-off. The difference is that with an AI colleague, every one of those three is written down where you can check it.
