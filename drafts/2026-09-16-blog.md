# What open-weight models mean for your AI workforce bill

If you've priced an AI tool lately, you've seen the pattern: a subscription, then usage on top, then a bill at month-end you didn't plan for. Token-based pricing puts the meter on your side of the glass. You're charged for the raw computation, and the raw computation depends on choices — model, prompt length, retries — you never made.

There's a second path, and it's the one Kohai runs on. The colleagues on your team — Mochi, your Strategy Lead, your Operations Lead — work exclusively on open-weight models. That single architectural choice changes three things at once: what happens to your data, what a piece of work costs, and how much of your AI stack you actually control.

## First, what "open-weight" actually means

An open-weight model is one whose trained parameters are published, so anyone can run it. DeepSeek, Kimi, and GLM are the workhorses here — frontier-adjacent models you can inspect, host, and route work across, rather than a proprietary API you rent by the token with no visibility into what changed under you last Tuesday.

The practical difference shows up in your data's journey. When your Sales & Growth Lead drafts outreach using your CRM notes, that work runs on open-weight models only — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — from certified no-training providers. Certified means the inference providers running the models are contractually barred from training on your data. Per-tenant isolation and encryption mean your workspace's work isn't commingled with anyone else's. Your client files stay yours — which, if you handle client financials, legal documents, or health-adjacent data, is the whole ballgame.

Compare that to the default in the category. Most AI-employee platforms run on frontier models or let you pick any model. Those are legitimate choices — more on that below — but the privacy posture is different. With a closed frontier API, the question "does our data train anything?" gets answered by a policy page. With open-weight and certified providers, it's answered by the architecture.

## Second: credits you can price before you brief

Open-weight models are also dramatically cheaper to run, and Kohai passes that through as fixed, visible pricing. You buy credits, not tokens. A blog post runs 20–50 credits. A deck runs 100–300. Before you assign work, you can see roughly what it costs — the way you'd know a freelance designer's day rate before handing over a project.

That's the difference between a meter you watch and a budget you set. On the entry plan, $29.99/month buys 12,000 credits and a 7-day free trial. "Pro tier: $99.99/mo for 40,000 credits and 3 seats" — sized for a small team where three people brief the same workforce. At Kohai's example pace, that's a week shipping 24 deliverables and reclaiming 31 hours, with the credit cost of each job legible up front. No retry-multiplication surprises, no per-token overage line.

For a solo operator or a lean team, predictability isn't a nice-to-have. It's the difference between delegating confidently and rationing your AI usage out of bill anxiety.

## Third: you're not locked to one vendor's roadmap

When your workforce runs on a single proprietary model, your product experience is that company's roadmap. Pricing changes, model deprecations, capability regressions — all of it lands on you, uninvited.

Open-weight routing inverts that. Work can move to whichever of the available open models best fits the job, and as the open-weight ecosystem improves — which it has, quickly — your workforce improves with it, without your briefs changing. You also gain a real answer to the dependency question every buyer should ask: if this vendor disappeared tomorrow, could the stack run elsewhere? With open weights, yes. With a proprietary API, no.

## Where we'll be generous to the alternatives

To be fair to the rest of the category, because they deserve it:

- **Relevance AI** lets you bring your own LLM keys, with vendor credits passed through at no markup — a genuinely transparent billing approach, and the closest analog to cost visibility in the space. If you want to build your own agents on top of that, it's a capable platform for it.
- **Lindy** is model-agnostic — you can pick from frontier models — and its credit-pause behavior (it stops rather than overcharges when a pool runs low) is an honest answer to bill anxiety. Its per-user pricing starts at the same $29.99 as Kohai's entry tier.
- **Artisan** runs its AI BDR, Ava, alongside human sales teams with campaign guardrails and approval modes — a thoughtful human-plus-AI posture for B2B outbound specifically.

None of them, though, runs exclusively on open-weight models with certified no-training providers and per-tenant isolation. That combination is the difference between "we have a privacy page" and "your data can't be trained on by design." If that distinction doesn't matter for your business, any of the above can serve you well. If it does — client data, regulated adjacent work, or just a principle you won't trade — it's the only one that does.

## What this means for how you brief

One underappreciated effect of open-weight economics: it changes your behavior as a delegator. When every token is a cost you can't predict, you write short prompts and avoid big asks. When a brief costs a known, small number of credits, you delegate the way you would to a person — the full outcome, in one message, including the parts you'd have skipped to save money.

That's the quiet argument for the whole architecture. Cheaper inference isn't just a lower bill. It's the precondition for briefing a colleague the way you'd brief a colleague — with the whole job, not a truncated version of it.

If your AI bill has ever been a surprise, or your data's journey through someone else's model has ever been a question mark, both problems trace back to the same root: a rented, closed stack. Open-weight colleagues are the alternative. Your first week is free to test — bring the brief you've been rationing.
