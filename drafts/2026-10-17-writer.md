# Meeting prep AI assistant: what Mochi does before, during, and after your meeting

Most "AI meeting assistants" show up after the meeting ends and hand you a transcript summary. That's the easiest part of the job — and the least valuable. The work that actually saves you time happens before you join the call and after you leave it.

Mochi, Kohai's Chief of Staff, runs a five-step meeting flow that covers the whole lifecycle: prep, brief, live notes, summary, follow-ups. This post walks through each step as it's documented on the product — what Mochi does, what you get, and where you stay in control. No case studies, no invented numbers. Just the flow, so you can decide if it fits how you run your week.

## What should a meeting prep AI assistant actually do?

It should get you ready, keep the record, and close the loop — in that order. A tool that only summarizes after the fact leaves you doing the two hardest parts yourself: walking in prepared and following through on what was said.

Mochi's flow is built around that full arc. The same colleague who prepped your meeting is the one who takes the notes and starts the follow-ups, so nothing gets lost in the handoff between three separate apps.

## The five steps, in order

Here is the flow as documented on Kohai's site:

1. **Preps the meeting.** Before the call, Mochi works through your connected tools — calendar, inbox, docs — and puts together the prep material for the meeting on your schedule.
2. **Briefs you 15 minutes before.** A short brief arrives a quarter hour ahead, so you walk in already knowing what the meeting is about and what you need from it.
3. **Takes live notes.** During the call, Mochi captures what's said while you actually pay attention to the person in front of you.
4. **Writes the summary.** After the meeting, the notes become a clean summary — a finished deliverable that lands in your Library, not a wall of raw transcript.
5. **Starts follow-ups.** The commitments made in the meeting turn into follow-up work. Sensitive follow-ups — anything that sends a message or commits you externally — wait at an approval gate until you say go.

The shape of that table matters: two steps happen before the meeting, one during, two after. Compare that to the typical tool, where four of five steps are "summarize it afterward."

## What you have to set up

One message. You brief Mochi the way you'd brief a capable hire — describe the meeting and the outcome you want from it, and the flow above is what runs. There's no workflow builder to drag boxes in and no prompt to engineer; the "recurring" version (a weekly pipeline review, a standing client check-in) is a schedule you set once.

Two details worth knowing:

- Mochi works inside your existing tools. Kohai connects to the apps you already use — Gmail, Calendar, Slack, Notion, Zoom and hundreds more — on day one, and each colleague only touches what you allow. You scope Mochi's tools; it doesn't get the keys to everything.
- Deliverables land in one place. The brief, the notes, and the summary all show up in your Library, so the record of a meeting isn't scattered across a chat thread and a doc somewhere.

## Where the approval gate comes in

The last step — follow-ups — is where a lot of AI tools would quietly overstep. An assistant that auto-sends a follow-up email on your behalf is making a promise in your voice, to a person you chose, at a time it picked.

Kohai's answer is the approval gate. Mochi drafts the follow-up, then stops. You review it and say go — or edit it, or kill it. For routine internal notes that might be a one-click approve; for a commitment made to a prospect or an investor, it's the difference between "AI did a thing" and "I did a thing, faster." You stay in control, and that's not a caveat — it's the design.

## Who this fits

If you run sales, content, ops, and support yourself, meetings are the worst possible place to lose an afternoon — you're in the call, then you pay the transcription tax, then you pay the follow-through tax. Mochi's five steps take the second and third tax off your plate and shrink the first one to a 15-minute brief.

If you lead a lean team, the same flow scales across everyone's calendars without adding a chief of staff to payroll — and the approval gate means you're not auditing autonomy, just approving drafts.

The 7-day free trial is the honest test: brief one real meeting, watch the flow run end to end, and judge the Library entry on Friday. If the summary and follow-ups aren't things you'd have written anyway, cancel — it's a trial, not a commitment.
