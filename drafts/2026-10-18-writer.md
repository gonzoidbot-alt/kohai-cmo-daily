# What to hand your AI team first: a solo founder's delegation order

An AI team for a solo founder is a set of named colleagues — a Chief of Staff, an Operations Lead, a Marketing & Comms Lead — who take recurring work off your calendar when you brief them the way you'd brief a capable hire. Hiring them is the easy part; the hard part is deciding what to hand over first.

Hand everything over at once and you'll spend your week auditing instead of building. Hand over nothing and you paid for a bench. This post gives you the order: four tasks, ranked, starting with the one that's safest to delegate this week.

## What makes a task safe to delegate first?

Three tests, and a task has to pass all of them:

| Test | What it means | Why it matters first |
|---|---|---|
| Recurring | It shows up every week whether you like it or not | One briefing pays off repeatedly |
| Verifiable | You can check the output in minutes, not hours | Trust gets built on checked work |
| Low blast radius | A mistake costs a redo, not a customer | Early errors stay cheap |

The Monday growth report passes all three. So does pulling together meeting notes or a status summary. Cold outbound to strangers does not — that's the last task on this list, not the first.

## The delegation order

Work down the list in order. Each step earns the trust the next one spends.

1. **The report.** Start with the Monday growth report — or whatever number you rebuild by hand every week. It's read-only, verifiable in minutes, and touches nobody outside your company. Brief it once, schedule it — Monday, 9:00 — and check the finished version in your Library before you'd have finished making it yourself.
2. **The chase.** Second is the follow-up work that leaks out of every week: invoices, unsent reminders, the "did you see my last email" list. Start with internal reminders, where the blast radius is near zero. Anything that goes to a client or a vendor waits at an approval gate — the draft arrives, you review, you say go.
3. **The queue.** Third is support triage. A Customer Support Lead can work the ticket queue around the clock — sorting, drafting, escalating — while the replies that actually go out sit behind your approval until you've seen enough of them to relax the scope.
4. **The pipeline.** Last is lead list to outreach sequence. This is the highest-value hand-off and the highest-stakes, because volume multiplies mistakes and every send speaks in your voice. Keep the approval gate on the sends longer than feels necessary; relax it when the drafts stop needing you.

Notice the shape: internal and verifiable first, external and sensitive last. Most failed AI rollouts run the order backwards — they start with the flashiest task, the one with the biggest external surface, and burn their trust on the first bad send.

## How the approval gate makes the order work

The approval gate is what lets you delegate in the first place. On Kohai, a sensitive action — a send, a commitment, a purchase — waits until you say go. You review the draft, approve or edit or kill it, and the work moves.

Two details make the ramp real:

- **Per-agent tool scoping.** Each colleague touches only the tools you allow. Your Operations Lead doesn't need your Stripe keys to build a status report, and doesn't get them.
- **You widen scope as trust earns it.** Week one, everything external waits for you. Week four, routine replies might not. The control is yours to loosen — it was never the vendor's to promise.

That's the difference between delegating and abdicating. You stay in control, and the design means it.

## What the week looks like once it's running

Once the schedule is set, the week runs on its own spine. Kohai's own example schedule reads like this: a growth report Monday at 9:00, a pipeline sweep Wednesday at 14:00, an invoice chase Friday at 16:00 — that last one flagged as needing approval. Set once, runs weekly.

And the output side is the part worth staring at. **Kohai's example week: 24 deliverables shipped, 31 hours reclaimed.** Twenty-four finished things — reports, summaries, drafts, sweeps — landed in the Library in a week, and thirty-one hours came back to the person who was doing all of it by hand.

Those are example figures, not a case study — treat them as the shape of a staffed week, not a promise. But the shape is the point: the hours you reclaim come exactly from the four tasks above, done by colleagues instead of by you at 9pm.

## What it costs to start

A 7-day free trial, cancel anytime. That's the honest test for a delegation order, because the order itself is free to run: brief one task on day one — the report, not the pipeline — and check whether a finished deliverable lands in your Library the same day.

If it does, hand over the chase next week and the queue the week after. If it doesn't, you've spent an afternoon, not a quarter. Either way you'll know more than the founder who bought five tools, integrated none of them, and is still writing the Monday report by hand.
