# How much should you budget for an AI employee?

If you're budgeting for a team of AI colleagues, the plans run $0, $20, $100, and $200 a month — and every plan includes unlimited seats. The more useful number is one layer down: what a deliverable costs, which you can see before you assign the work.

This post prices both layers using the figures published on kohai.co/pricing, verified 2026-10-19. The goal is a budget line you can write down in advance — not an invoice you explain afterward.

## What do the plans cost?

Four tiers, and the tiers differ by credit pool, not by features locked behind a wall:

| Plan | Monthly price | Credits |
|---|---|---|
| Kōhai Free | $0 | Credits refresh monthly — limited-time for the first 1,000 users |
| Kōhai | $20/mo | 8,000 |
| Kōhai Pro | $100/mo | 40,000 |
| Kōhai Max | $200/mo | 80,000 |

Every plan carries the whole team, every role template, and every integration. Unlimited seats on every plan means the bill doesn't grow when a teammate joins — a five-person company and a solo founder on the same tier pay the same. A 7-day free trial runs first, then drops to the Free plan; no card needed to start.

## What does the work itself cost?

Kohai prices work in credits, not tokens. Each deliverable has a published range, so you can cost a task before you assign it:

| Task size | Examples | Credits |
|---|---|---|
| Small | An email draft, a quick lookup | 5–10 |
| Medium | A chart, a landing page, a blog post | 20–50 |
| Large | A deck, a piece of deep research | 100–300 |

One rule changes how you budget: failed tasks consume 0 credits. You pay for finished work, not attempts. Add-on credits never expire, and your plan's credits refresh monthly — so a quiet month doesn't burn money and a busy one doesn't blindside it.

## What does a week of the work actually cost?

The published example week is the budgeting unit: **"Kohai's example week: 24 deliverables shipped, 31 hours reclaimed."** Run the arithmetic forward and a month of example weeks looks like this — illustrative math from published numbers, not a promise about your usage:

| Line | Figure |
|---|---|
| Deliverables per week | 24 |
| Weeks per month | ~4.3 |
| Deliverables per month | ~104 |
| Average credits per deliverable (mid-range) | ~50 |
| Estimated monthly usage | ~5,200 credits |
| Plan that covers it | Kōhai ($20/mo, 8,000 credits) |

Even weighting every deliverable at the top of the medium band, the example month fits inside the $20 tier with headroom for the large pieces. The $100 and $200 tiers exist for teams running several workstreams at volume — sales outreach, content, ops reporting, and support triage all recurring weekly — not because the per-deliverable math requires them.

Your mix will differ from the example week; that's the point of the table above. Cost your own five recurring deliverables against the ranges, and the plan picks itself.

## Why can the bill be predictable at all?

Two design choices make a pre-written budget possible instead of a guess.

The first is credits. A token-metered API bill reprices every task after the model runs — the same request can cost different amounts depending on how the model rambled. A credit-priced deliverable has a range attached before it starts, and a zero on failure.

The second is the model stack. Kohai runs exclusively on open-weight models — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — sourced from inference providers certified not to train on your data, under per-tenant isolation with encryption. Open-weight models are the reason the underlying costs stay steady rather than riding whatever a frontier lab prices its flagship at this quarter. Steady inputs are what make steady outputs priceable; the credit ranges would wobble if the foundation did.

## What pushes the bill up — and what holds it down?

The levers are few and visible:

- **Pushes up:** more large deliverables (decks, deep research at 100–300 credits), and more recurring schedules running in parallel.
- **Holds down:** 0-credit failures, monthly refresh on unused pool, non-expiring add-on credits, and unlimited seats — adding a teammate never adds a line.
- **You set the ceiling:** recurring work runs on schedules you created — a growth report Monday at 9:00, an invoice chase Friday at 16:00 — and anything sensitive waits at an approval gate before it acts.

There's no usage you didn't configure and no seat you didn't invite. The bill is a function of the work you asked for.

## How does $100 a month compare to the alternatives?

Two comparisons, both directional rather than exact.

Against a tool stack: the usual solo-operator stack is seven or eight point solutions, most priced per seat, each needing setup and each with its own meter. Consolidating the work those tools cover into one workforce line replaces a shelf of meters with one number you can read.

Against a hire: the top plan costs less per month than a single day of a part-time coordinator. That's not a claim that colleagues replace people — it's the reason the budget conversation is short. The line item is small enough that the question becomes "what did the work deliver," not "can we afford the seat." That's the same case 06's finance playbook makes: keep the spend below the veto threshold and the argument stays about outcomes.

## How do you set the budget in one afternoon?

1. **List your recurring deliverables** — the report, the chase, the queue, the content, the pipeline sweep.
2. **Tag each one small, medium, or large** using the credit table above.
3. **Estimate the month** — count per deliverable times weeks running, at the mid-range credit figure.
4. **Pick the plan one band above your estimate.** Headroom is cheaper than a mid-month top-up decision.
5. **Re-run the math after 30 days** on real usage, and right-size once — not weekly.

Start on the Free plan — no card — and brief one deliverable today. When the first finished work lands in your Library the same day, you'll have both numbers that matter: what it cost, and what the hour it replaced was worth.
