# You already know how to manage an AI team

Somewhere in the last year, adopting AI at work turned into a job listing. Build workflows. Craft briefs that machines follow. Wire triggers to actions. Maintain the whole contraption when a tool changes its API. Founders read this and quietly conclude that using AI well requires becoming a different kind of person — a technical one.

It doesn't. There's a version of working with AI that uses skills you already practice every week: hiring people, briefing them, delegating, reviewing the work, and deciding what needs your sign-off. If you've ever managed a freelancer, an intern, or a first employee, you're already qualified.

## Hiring is a skill you have. Building is one you'd have to learn.

When you hire a person, you don't write their job as a flowchart. You describe the outcome — "own the books, chase anything past 30 days, flag anything weird" — and trust them to figure out the steps. You check their early work closely, then less. You give them access to what they need and not what they don't.

Briefing an AI colleague works the same way. On Kohai, you describe the outcome in one message, and Mochi, your Chief of Staff, splits it across the right specialists — a Sales & Growth Lead, an Operations Lead, a Marketing & Comms Lead — each with the tools for their job already connected. You never assemble the machinery, because the machinery isn't yours. Your job is the part you've done before: saying what "done" looks like.

Agent-builder platforms — Lindy and Relevance AI are the serious ones in this category — ask for something else entirely: a craft. Node canvases, trigger logic, error handling. These are good tools, and if systems-building is your idea of a good time, they'll reward the investment. But for a founder whose actual work is clients, product, and sales, that's a second apprenticeship on top of a full calendar.

## What the brief looks like

Here's a real brief, the kind you'd hand a capable new hire on day one:

> "Go through our last 90 days of Stripe payments, find customers we haven't spoken to since their purchase, and draft a short check-in for each — something a human would actually send. Queue them for my approval before anything goes out."

One message. No nodes, no trigger configuration, no decision tree to diagram. The drafting happens, the drafts land in your Library as finished work, and the send sits at an approval gate until you say go — because customer-facing messages are sensitive, and you stay in control of those.

Notice what you did and didn't do. You didn't specify which tools to use, in what order, or what happens when a record is missing. You stated the outcome, the tone, and the one judgment call that's yours. That's delegation, and you've done it a hundred times with people.

## The management moves that transfer directly

Every habit from managing humans has a counterpart here:

| What you do with a new hire | What you do with an AI colleague |
|---|---|
| Write a job description | Write one brief — same sentence, no diagram |
| Onboard them onto your tools | Scope which tools each teammate can touch |
| Delegate a recurring task | Set a schedule: "Mon 9:00, growth report" |
| Review their first deliverable | Read it in your Library |
| Keep sign-off on sensitive decisions | Approval gates, by default |
| Give follow-up instructions | Re-brief in one sentence |

The last row is the underrated one. When a person's work misses the mark, you don't rewrite their job description — you give feedback. Same here: when a deliverable isn't right, you re-brief, and the next version reflects it. You're never debugging a workflow you built; you're managing a colleague, which is the skill you already had.

## What the first week of hiring looks like

So what does this management style produce in practice, once the briefs are running? "Kohai's example week: 24 deliverables shipped, 31 hours reclaimed." That's outreach sequences drafted, a weekly growth report written, support tickets triaged, follow-ups sent — the recurring work that used to fill your evenings, now landing as finished artifacts you review instead of tasks you perform.

The deliverables aren't limited to documents, either. Ask for a landing page or an internal tool and it arrives built, live, and hosted — "App Studio ships deployed software, not mockups." You briefed an outcome; the finished thing exists in the world. That's the standard a hire is held to, and it's the standard here.

For a solo founder, 31 hours is most of a workweek. It's the difference between ending Friday with everything touched and ending it with the important things done.

## When you should still build

The honest exception, stated plainly: if building automation is your product or your craft — you're a consultant who ships workflows, or you genuinely enjoy the builder's canvas — then build. Lindy and Relevance AI will serve you well and are built by people who take that craft seriously. Kohai isn't for the person who loves the machinery. It's for the person who tried the machinery, felt the maintenance bill, and realized what they wanted was a colleague.

## Make your first hire this week

Pick the one recurring task that eats your Monday. Brief it the way you'd brief a person on their first day: the outcome, the tone, the decisions that stay yours. The 7-day free trial covers the whole experiment, and the first deliverable is usually in your Library the same day.

You've onboarded people before. This one just starts faster.
