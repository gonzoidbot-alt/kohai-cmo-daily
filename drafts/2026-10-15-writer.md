# Is there an Artisan alternative for small teams that covers more than outbound?

Artisan's flagship "Artisan," Ava, is an AI business-development rep: she finds leads, enriches them, runs multi-channel outreach sequences, handles replies, and books meetings. If your one burning problem is pipeline, that's a serious product — and this is not a takedown.

But small teams rarely have exactly one problem. The search for an Artisan alternative usually starts the day you notice the tool covers Tuesday's job and nothing else: the ops work, the content, the support queue, and the Monday report are still yours. This post compares the two shapes honestly — the vertical specialist and the pre-staffed team — and gives you a one-week test for deciding which your business actually needs.

## What is Artisan genuinely good at?

Depth in one motion. Ava is built for B2B outbound end to end: a large B2B contact database, enrichment from dozens of sources, multi-channel sequences, automated message testing, reply handling, and meeting booking — with campaign guardrails (approval modes, escalation rules, do-not-call lists) and a human-rep layer for the calls AI shouldn't make. The company has raised real venture money, publishes enterprise-grade security commitments, and serves thousands of sales teams.

If outbound sales is the work that's underwater, a dedicated AI BDR is a legitimate answer, and Artisan is one of the established ones. The trade-off isn't quality — it's scope.

## What is a vertical AI hire not designed to do?

Everything outside its vertical, by design. Ava runs outbound; she is not positioned as your operations lead, your content writer, or your support triage. That narrowness is a feature for the buyer who wants exactly one job done well, and a gap for the buyer whose constraint is the whole week.

The structural difference is worth naming precisely: a vertical product optimizes one workflow and goes deep on it; a horizontal product covers many workflows and goes broad on all of them. Neither shape is wrong. But the two shapes answer different questions — "who runs my pipeline?" versus "who runs my week?" — and the misbuy happens when a buyer answers one question while holding the other in mind.

## How does a pre-staffed AI team compare on scope?

Where a vertical hire answers the pipeline question, a pre-staffed team answers the week question. On Kohai, you don't hire a single specialist — you get a staffed roster: Mochi as Chief of Staff coordinating inbox, calendar, and follow-ups, plus named leads for sales and growth, marketing and comms, support, and operations. You brief an outcome; the team splits it; finished work lands in your Library.

Here's the same week, sorted by who covers it:

| Job in your week | Vertical AI hire (AI BDR) | Pre-staffed AI team |
|---|---|---|
| Outbound prospecting and sequences | Core job — deep and specialized | Covered by the Sales & Growth Lead, breadth-first |
| Meeting prep, notes, follow-ups | Not the product's job | Chief of Staff's core loop |
| Weekly growth and ops reports | Not the product's job | Operations Lead, on a schedule |
| Blog posts, decks, SEO content | Not the product's job | Marketing & Comms Lead |
| Support inbox triage | Not the product's job | Support Lead |
| Small custom tools and pages | Not the product's job | App Studio (see below) |

Read the two columns as two different purchases. The left column buys excellence at one row. The right column buys coverage of the whole table, accepting that no single row goes as deep as a dedicated vertical tool.

## Where do the two models part ways on control and data?

Both camps take control seriously, so the differences are worth checking rather than assumed. Artisan ships campaign guardrails — approval modes, escalation rules, suppression lists — which is a real control story for outbound specifically. Kohai's approval gate is horizontal: any step you flag as sensitive — sends, payments, outbound messages, anything with outside impact — waits for your go, in any workstream, not just sequences.

On the model layer, ask both vendors the same two questions: which models run the work, and do the providers train on your data. Kohai's stack is open-weight only (DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM) from certified no-training providers, with per-tenant isolation — a checkable answer a client-data business can put in front of its own clients. Whichever shape you buy, that question belongs on the shortlist.

## What does each shape cost, structurally?

Artisan's published self-serve tiers run from a small free credit allotment up through paid plans priced in the hundreds of dollars monthly (their own page is sales-led, so confirm current tiers with them directly), with the dialer as a per-seat add-on. The vertical's honest cost structure: pricing that scales with campaign volume and seats, aimed at teams whose main spend line is pipeline generation.

A pre-staffed team prices the week, not the campaign: one flat monthly tier covering all the roles, with work metered in credits you can see per task before you assign it — a blog post and a pipeline sweep quote differently, and both quote up front. Whichever direction you lean, the comparison to run is cost per finished deliverable across your whole week, not sticker price on the one row that hurts most. Kohai's entry tier and its 7-day free trial are on the pricing page; we keep this post number-free so the numbers you compare come from the live page, not a blog post written last week.

## Can a horizontal team still cover the custom-software want?

Often the reason a founder drifts toward a vertical or a builder is a specific custom want — a client portal, a quote calculator, a lead-magnet page — that neither staffed rosters nor BDR tools traditionally addressed. Kohai's answer is on the record: "App Studio ships deployed software, not mockups." You describe the tool; what comes back is built, deployed, and hosted — a running application next to the reports and follow-ups in your Library.

That's the section where the horizontal shape stops being a compromise. If your shortlist question is "which product covers my pipeline," you're comparing tools. If it's "which product covers my week and builds the odd custom thing," the shapes stop overlapping at all.

## How do you decide in one week?

Run the test against your real calendar, not a demo script:

1. **Write down your five recurring jobs** — the report, the chase, the queue, the content, the pipeline. Rank them by hours, not urgency.
2. **If one job holds most of the hours,** trial the vertical. Depth where the hours are beats coverage where they aren't, and Artisan's 30-day trial exists for exactly that test.
3. **If the hours are spread across four or five jobs,** trial the team. Brief two tasks from different rows of the table on day one, and see whether both deliverables arrive finished.
4. **Score the same three numbers on both** — time to first finished deliverable, minutes you spent setting up, minutes you spent reviewing.
5. **Let the numbers pick the shape.** The product that wins is the one whose scope matches your week — and if that's the vertical, buy it with confidence.

A narrow tool bought on purpose is a good purchase. A narrow tool bought by accident is a month you don't get back.
