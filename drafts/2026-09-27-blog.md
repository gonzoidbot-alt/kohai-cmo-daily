# Why your AI bill shouldn't be a surprise

The first month with a token-billed AI tool goes fine. The second month the bill doubles, and nobody can say which task did it. That's not a discipline problem on your side — it's what happens when software prices its work in a unit you can't see, on a meter you don't control.

There's a different way to buy AI work, and it's the way you already buy everything else: a fixed monthly cost, and a stated price per piece of work before you assign it. This post is about how that works — the open-weight models that make it possible, and the credit systems that make it budgetable.

## Why do token bills swing so much?

Tokens are the raw material of language models, and usage-based billing passes their cost straight to you. The problem is that you don't control the inputs: a verbose model, a long document, a tool that re-reads the same file five times — each silently multiplies the meter. You find out what a task cost after the task, on an invoice.

Per-task pricing inverts that. You see what a blog post, a deck, or a pipeline sweep costs before you brief it, and the monthly plan caps the total. The bill stops being a mystery because it was never a meter.

## What are open-weight models, and why do they matter for cost?

Open-weight models are language models whose weights are published — anyone can run them, and anyone can compete on running them well. Kohai runs exclusively on open-weight models: DeepSeek, Kimi, and GLM. Because the models aren't captive to one lab's pricing, inference stays competitive, and that competition is what makes flat per-task credit pricing possible at all.

There's a privacy dividend on the same rail: Kohai's inference providers are certified not to train on your data, and per-tenant isolation keeps your work separated from every other customer's. The cost story and the privacy story are the same story — open models, run by providers who compete on trust, priced by the task rather than the token.

## How do credits compare to tokens and hours?

Three pricing mechanics dominate this category, and each one changes what your month looks like. The differences are worth seeing side by side.

| | Token-billed tools | Credit-based platforms | Hours-included platforms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Billing unit | Model input/output, invisible until invoiced | Stated per-task costs from a fixed monthly pool | A monthly allowance of work time |
| Predictability | Low — scales with usage you don't control | High — plan price caps the month | High — flat, all-inclusive |
| What a task costs upfront | Unknown | Known before you assign it | Rough (hours don't say what fits) |
| Failure mode | A bill that doubles in month two | Running low near month's end | Ambiguity about what "one hour" buys |

Credit systems and hours systems both solve the surprise-bill problem. Credits do it with more granularity: a blog post runs 20–50 credits, a deck 100–300, so you can rank your backlog by cost before you spend anything. That ranking is what turns an AI subscription from a leap of faith into a line item you defend in a budget meeting.

## What does a real plan look like?

Kohai's entry point: "First AI hire: $29.99/month, 12,000 credits, 7-day free trial." That covers a solo operator's week — a growth report, some content drafts, a pipeline sweep — with the plan price fixed before the month starts.

When the work outgrows one seat, the step up is the tier this post's math rests on: "Pro tier: $99.99/mo for 40,000 credits and 3 seats." Three seats at $99.99 means roughly $33 per person per month for a shared 40,000-credit pool — the whole team's AI colleagues draw from one visible balance instead of three separate meters.

The number worth holding onto is the ratio: 40,000 credits at the Pro tier buys on the order of a hundred decks or several hundred blog posts in a month. Whatever platform you choose, ask for that same translation — allowance in, work out — before you sign.

## How do the alternatives handle this?

Credit where it's due, because several platforms have thought hard about the same problem. Lindy pauses when your credit pool runs low rather than overcharging — a decent answer to the surprise-bill problem, and it states it plainly. Relevance AI passes model costs through at no markup and lets you bring your own API keys, which is the most transparent pass-through economics in the category. Marblism sidesteps meters entirely with hours included in a flat price.

Kohai's wager is that transparency should start before the work, not on the invoice: open-weight models keep the underlying cost competitive, credits state the price per task, and the plan caps the month. Those are three mechanisms pointed at the same outcome — a bill you could have predicted.

## Pick one task and price it

You don't need to trust this math; you need to run it once. Take the recurring task that eats your week — the Monday report, the invoice chase — and find its credit cost before you brief it. The 7-day free trial exists precisely so the first budget conversation happens with real numbers.

Predictability isn't a feature you notice on a landing page. It's the absence of a feeling you get on invoice day — and it's worth choosing for.
