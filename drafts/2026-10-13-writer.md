# How to delegate ops to AI without building a single workflow

If you run a business with fewer than five people, operations are the reason your week is full before real work starts. Growth reports, pipeline sweeps, invoice chases, meeting notes, follow-ups — necessary, repetitive, and never done.

The usual advice is to automate it: pick a workflow builder, draw the diagram, connect the nodes, maintain the automations forever. That trades one kind of work for another. There is a second option — brief an AI colleague the way you'd brief a new hire, and let the delegation happen in one message.

## What does "delegating ops to AI" actually mean?

It means handing a recurring outcome to an AI teammate, not wiring up a process. You describe what done looks like; the work gets planned, executed in your existing tools, and delivered to you as finished files.

The distinction matters because it changes where your time goes. With workflow builders, your time goes into building and maintaining the machine. With a colleague model, your time goes into reviewing finished work — and approving the handful of steps that touch anything sensitive.

## The five-step playbook

This is the same brief-then-review loop for every recurring task. Once you've done it once, each new handoff takes minutes.

1. **Pick one recurring task that eats a fixed slot.** The Monday growth report, the Wednesday pipeline sweep, the Friday invoice chase. Start with one — the point is to prove the loop, not to delegate everything at once.
2. **Describe the outcome, not the steps.** "Every Monday at 9:00, pull last week's numbers and draft the growth report I usually send" is the whole job description. You are briefing a colleague, so you talk about the result you want — the colleague figures out the steps.
3. **Set the schedule once.** Recurring work is the core case: set it for Monday 9:00 and it runs weekly without you touching it again. Event triggers work too, but schedules are where most operator hours hide.
4. **Mark the sensitive parts.** Anything that spends money, sends on your behalf, or touches customer relationships waits at an approval gate until you say go. You decide which steps those are when you set the task up.
5. **Review the deliverable, not the process.** Finished work lands in your Library — docs, spreadsheets, reports. Your weekly job shrinks from *doing* the report to *reading* it.

## Where the approval gate fits

Delegation fails when you can't see what the machine is about to do on your behalf. The approval gate is the control point: the colleague does the drafting, the research, the prep — and pauses before any action you flagged as sensitive.

The invoice chase is the canonical example. The colleague can chase, draft the reminder, track who responded — but the actual send waits for your go. That's the difference between "automation ran overnight" and "I stayed in control overnight."

## What the team looks like

You're not managing one generic assistant. Kohai assigns named roles: Mochi as your Chief of Staff handles inbox, calendar, meeting prep, and follow-ups; separate leads cover sales and growth, marketing and comms, support, and operations. Each colleague only touches the tools you've scoped for it — Gmail, Slack, Notion, Stripe, and roughly a thousand others connect on day one, with per-agent scoping so each hire sees only what you allow.

## What it costs to start

The entry tier is $29.99/month for 12,000 credits, with a 7-day free trial. Credits price work before you assign it — a blog post runs 20–50 credits, a deck 100–300 — so a recurring task's cost is knowable up front rather than a monthly surprise.

For a solo operator, that math is the whole pitch: the first delegated task probably costs less than the hour it used to take you, and the trial tells you whether it's real before you pay anything.

## Start with one task this week

Pick the task that most reliably ruins a good morning. Brief it in one message, set the schedule, mark the sensitive step, and read the first deliverable instead of writing it. If the loop holds, add the next task the following week — and by the end of the month, the recurring layer of your operations belongs to someone else.
