# How to chase unpaid invoices without writing another awkward email

Every service business has a list it doesn't talk about. Invoices sent, polite, and unpaid. Week one you let it go. Week two you mean to follow up. Week three the nudge feels rude, so it waits until Friday afternoon — and by then the client has paid two other vendors first.

This isn't a discipline problem. It's a delegation problem. Chasing money you're owed is the exact kind of work you should never be doing by hand: it recurs every week, it follows a script you already know, and the only part that genuinely needs you is the go/no-go on anything that touches a client relationship. That shape — routine work with one sensitive step — is what recurring AI colleagues were made for.

Here's the playbook.

## What does "automated invoice chase" actually have to mean?

The bar is simple: on Friday afternoon, the follow-ups for every overdue invoice are drafted, sorted, and waiting for your approval — and you wrote none of them.

A mail-merge template doesn't clear that bar. Templates send the same day-30 email to a client who's 90 days late and a client who simply missed a notification, and they send without anyone deciding whether this week is the week to escalate. What you want is the judgment, not just the email: who to nudge, how firmly, in what tone, and when to stop nudging and escalate to a call. Software can do all of that as a first draft. The send button stays yours.

## The setup: one brief, one schedule, one gate

You brief this the way you'd brief a competent office manager on their first week — the outcome, not the steps:

1. **Brief the outcome.** "Every Friday at 16:00, review unpaid invoices in Stripe and Xero. For anything overdue, draft a follow-up in the client's tone history — friendly at 7 days, firmer at 30, a clear escalation path at 60. Queue them for my approval." One message is the whole job description. If you'd have to explain it to a new hire anyway, you can brief it.
2. **Let the team assemble itself.** On Kohai, Mochi, your Chief of Staff, splits that brief across the right specialists — your Operations Lead picks up the reporting and drafting, with the right tools (Stripe, your inbox, your spreadsheet) already connected. You never wire a step.
3. **Scope the tools.** The ops hire gets your billing tools and drafts folder — not your whole workspace. Per-agent tool scoping is a two-minute decision at setup and the reason you never think about it after.
4. **Set the schedule once.** "Fri 16:00 — Invoice chase" is a saved schedule. It runs weekly without being re-briefed.
5. **Put the gate on the send.** This is the whole trick. Every drafted nudge waits at an approval gate until you say go. Nothing leaves to your best client — or your slowest one — without you.

Setup is one honest conversation. After that, the chase runs whether or not you remember it exists.

## What lands in your Library on Friday

Not a chat transcript — finished work:

- **The chase list.** Every open invoice, aged, sorted by who to contact first and why.
- **Drafted nudges, individually written.** The 7-day reminder for the client who's usually prompt. The firmer note for the one who's been quiet since June. Each ready to approve or edit in one line.
- **The escalation shortlist.** The handful that need a phone call from you, with a two-line history so you walk in informed.

You spend ten minutes approving. The two hours you'd have spent drafting and dreading go back to billable work.

## The aging report you don't have to build

Most businesses that chase invoices seriously eventually want a proper AR view: aging buckets, average days-to-pay per client, who quietly always pays at 45. The usual options are a spreadsheet you maintain by hand or a reporting project you never start.

There's a third path now. Ask your team for the dashboard — "build me an AR aging report, live from Stripe, one page" — and it gets built, deployed, and hosted. "App Studio ships deployed software, not mockups." You get a URL you can open every Friday morning before the chase list lands, not a mockup you'd have to turn into software yourself. The follow-up tooling around a cash-flow problem shouldn't be a project; now it's a sentence in a brief.

## What does running it cost?

"First AI hire: $29.99/month, 12,000 credits, 7-day free trial." A weekly invoice sweep is a small slice of that budget — a fraction of a single late payment, which is the honest comparison. If the chase recovers even one invoice a quarter that would otherwise have drifted another month, the first hire has paid for itself many times over. And because you can size work in credits before you brief it, the bill is never a surprise at month-end.

## How do you stay in control once it runs without you?

You handed over the typing, not the judgment. The drafts are yours to edit line by line — the fix for a wrong tone is a sentence, not a configuration change. Scoped tools mean the ops hire only ever saw billing and drafts. And every send sits at the gate: no email reaches a client until you've seen it.

That last part isn't a limitation to apologize for. A payment reminder to your best client is exactly the kind of message a human should approve, and any automation honest enough to say so is the kind you'll actually trust to run every week.

## Start Friday

Pick the boring, money-shaped job first. Brief the chase once, set the schedule, approve the first week's drafts personally, and let it recur. The awkward email gets written every week from now on — just never again by you.
