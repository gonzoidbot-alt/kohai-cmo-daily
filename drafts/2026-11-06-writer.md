# Predictable AI Pricing: Why the Billing Model Matters More Than the Price

**Target query:** AI tools with predictable pricing
**Meta description:** Token-metered AI bills swing month to month. Per-deliverable credit pricing tells you what each task costs before you assign it. Here's how to budget it.

If you've ever opened an AI invoice and felt your stomach drop, the problem probably wasn't the price. It was the *shape* of the price — a meter that ticked with every token, every seat, every API call, and no way to know in advance what a week of work would cost.

The good news: "predictable" isn't a pricing tier, it's a billing model. And once you can tell the three common models apart, you can budget AI the way you budget anything else — with a number you trust before the month starts.

## The three ways AI tools bill you

Most AI products use one of three billing shapes. Each one answers a different question about who carries the risk when usage varies — and that's the whole predictability question in disguise.

| Billing model | What you pay for | When the bill surprises you |
|---|---|---|
| **Token/usage metering** | The raw compute behind every prompt | Every time usage spikes — a busy week, a long document, a chatty workflow |
| **Per-seat SaaS** | Each human user, monthly | Every time the team grows — even if the new seat barely uses the tool |
| **Credit-per-deliverable** | Finished work, priced by size | Rarely — the cost of a task is known *before* you assign it |

Token metering puts usage risk on you: the vendor's revenue is identical whether your task took one try or ten. Per-seat pricing puts headcount risk on you: you pay for the seat, not the output. Credit-per-deliverable flips both — the platform carries the variance, and you see the price of the work up front.

## What "priced by the deliverable" looks like

Under a credit model, work has a published price band before you assign it. A small task — a short email, a data lookup — costs a handful of credits. A medium deliverable — a blog post, a landing page, a chart — costs in the tens. A large one — a full deck, a research brief — costs in the low hundreds.

Two details make the model genuinely predictable rather than just theoretically so:

1. **Failed tasks cost nothing.** If a task errors out, the credits come back or never leave. You're paying for finished work, not for attempts.
2. **Credits refresh monthly, and add-on credits don't expire.** A quiet month doesn't burn your balance, and a busy month doesn't force you into a bigger plan you'll regret next quarter.

The result is a budget line you can actually write down. If you know roughly what your team produces in a week — a few small tasks, two or three blog posts, a deck — you can multiply it out and land within a plan tier *before* you sign up, not after the first invoice.

## Doing the math on a real budget

Here's the exercise we'd suggest for any AI tool, whatever its billing model:

1. **List your recurring deliverables** — the weekly report, the content calendar, the support triage, the outreach sequence. Count them honestly.
2. **Price each one.** Under a deliverable model, the price is published. Under a token or seat model, estimate from last month's usage — and add a buffer for the spike week you know is coming.
3. **Compare the monthly totals against the plans.** For reference, Kōhai's current plans (verified on kohai.co/pricing) run Free at $0 — no card, limited time — then $20/mo for 8,000 credits, $100/mo Pro for 40,000, and $200/mo Max for 80,000, with unlimited team seats on every plan. Unlimited seats matter for the math: a five-person team doesn't pay five times; the deliverables are the unit, not the people.

Run the same three steps on any platform you're evaluating. If step 2 makes you guess, that's the tell — a pricing page that can't tell you what a deliverable costs is a pricing page that's asking you to absorb the variance.

## Why flat and predictable beats "cheaper but variable"

A lower list price with an unpredictable meter is often the more expensive choice in practice. The surprise isn't just the dollars — it's the decisions the uncertainty forces. Teams under usage metering ration their own AI usage: they skip the second draft, the extra report, the follow-up sequence, because each one moves a meter they can't see the end of. Predictable pricing does the opposite — it makes the marginal task free to attempt, because the plan already covers it and failed attempts cost nothing.

That's also why "your AI bill shouldn't be a surprise" is a design principle, not a slogan. A team that knows what work costs assigns more of it. A team that doesn't, hesitates — and hesitation is the hidden cost no pricing page advertises.

## Try the math with zero commitment

The cheapest way to test whether a pricing model actually holds up in practice is to run it at $0. On Kōhai: **"Start on Kōhai Free — $0, no card, for a limited time — and your first AI teammates are working today."** Brief one recurring deliverable, watch what it costs in credits, and you'll know exactly what your month looks like before you ever enter a card number.

Predictability, it turns out, starts at zero.
