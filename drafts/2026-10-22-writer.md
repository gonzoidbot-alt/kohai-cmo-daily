# How to run a content calendar with an AI marketing lead

An AI content calendar is a weekly schedule of content work — research, briefs, drafts, repurposing, and a performance readout — run by a named colleague and reviewed by you. You brief the Marketing & Comms Lead once, set the schedule, and the week's content shows up as finished deliverables instead of open tabs.

This post is the playbook for that week: what the calendar covers, which parts stay yours, and how to keep the output sounding like you. It's written for the operator doing content personally who wants the work to keep running without becoming another tool to manage.

## What does an AI content calendar actually cover?

Four recurring workstreams, one deliverable each:

| Recurring slot | What the Marketing & Comms Lead produces | Where you check it |
|---|---|---|
| Research & briefs | Topics drawn from your market, your customers, and last week's numbers — each one a working title plus the reader it serves | Library |
| Drafts | Posts, emails, and social copy written against those briefs | Library |
| Repurposing | Each draft split into the formats you actually use | Library |
| Performance readout | What shipped, what got read, what's worth repeating | Library |

The last column is the point. Every slot ends in a finished deliverable in one place, not a chat thread you have to mine. A calendar tool that produces reminders instead of deliverables has moved the work, not done it.

## Which parts of content stay yours?

Two things: the judgment and the shipping. The colleague runs the process; you own the decisions.

| Runs on the schedule | Waits for you |
|---|---|
| Research, topic selection, first drafts, repurposing, the weekly readout | Positioning and opinions, anything customer-facing, the publish button |

Positioning stays yours because it's a decision, not a task — what you believe and how you sound can't be delegated to a tool that wasn't in the room when the business took shape. And everything external waits at an approval gate: the draft arrives, you review it, you say go. A calendar can run itself; your voice and your send button don't hand themselves over.

## How do you set the calendar up?

Four steps, one sitting:

1. **Brief the outcome, not the posts.** "Keep one post and two emails moving every week for our audience" is a job description. A list of ten half-written headlines is a to-do list you've just forwarded.
2. **Name the sources.** Where does the truth live — customer calls, support tickets, product metrics, your own notes? The colleague works from what you point at, so point at real things.
3. **Set the schedule once.** Briefs early in the week, drafts midweek, the readout Friday. Set it like you'd set any standing meeting and let it recur without re-asking.
4. **Define what's sensitive.** Drafts only — nothing publishes without you. That single constraint is what makes the rest of the automation safe to accept.

A brief that works, in one line: *"One page of post briefs every Monday: three topics from what customers asked this week, each with a working title, the one reader it serves, and the number it leads with. Drafts only — I send."*

## What does a running week look like?

Once the schedule is set, the calendar runs on connections that already exist — **~1,000 integrations connected day one, with per-agent tool scoping.** The Marketing & Comms Lead reads the sources you named, drafts against the briefs, and drops finished work into your Library with no integration project in between. Scoping matters here too: your marketing colleague touches the tools content needs, and stays out of your books.

By Friday the Library holds the week — briefs, drafts, the repurposed variants, and the readout. On the site's own example scale, a fully staffed week across sales, content, ops, and support ships 24 deliverables and reclaims 31 hours, with content alone priced at eight hours a week of founder time. Those are example figures, not a case study — treat them as the shape of a staffed week, not a promise.

## How do you keep the calendar sounding like you?

The first week's drafts will miss. Correct them in one sentence — shorter, lead with the number, drop the jargon — and let the corrected brief stand as the new standing instruction. That's the compounding part of the calendar: every correction is permanent, so week four's drafts start where week one's ended.

Widen the scope as trust earns it. Week one, everything waits for your approval. Week four, the internal repurposing might not need a look, while anything customer-facing still does. The control is yours to loosen on your evidence — never the vendor's to promise.

Start on the Free plan — no card — and brief one week of content today. If Friday's Library looks like a calendar you'd have written yourself, you've bought the hours back. If it doesn't, you've spent an afternoon finding out.
