# The AI work that runs itself: how recurring automation actually works

You brief a colleague once, the work lands in your Library, and it's good — and then next Monday the same task is back on your list, waiting to be briefed again. That second part is the mistake. The work you do every week is exactly the work you should never have to set up twice.

Recurring AI tasks are the fix: you write the brief once, attach it to a schedule or a trigger, and the same work runs on its own from then on. Most people never use this layer, because they don't know it exists — they treat AI as something you ask for each time, like a search bar, when the recurring half of their week behaves more like a subscription.

## What counts as a recurring AI task?

Anything you do on a clock or in response to an event, where the shape of the output barely changes week to week. The Monday growth report, the Wednesday pipeline sweep, the Friday invoice chase, the support queue triaged every morning — these are schedules. A new lead arriving, a refund request landing, a contract approaching renewal — those are triggers, and they fire the same brief whenever the event happens.

The test is simple: if you could write the instructions for next week's version today, it's a candidate. If every run needs fresh judgment about what the work even is, it isn't — yet. Start with the first kind.

## What does one automation actually consist of?

Five parts, and you've already written most of them if you've delegated the task once. Here's the anatomy, using the weekly pipeline sweep as the worked example:

| Part | What it is | Pipeline-sweep example |
|---|---|---|
| **Trigger** | What starts the run — a time or an event | Wednesdays at 14:00 |
| **Brief** | The outcome, audience, constraints, and check — written once | "Update deal stages from last week's activity; flag every deal with no touch in 14 days; output as a one-page summary" |
| **Colleague** | The named role that owns the work | Your Sales & Growth Lead, with CRM access |
| **Approval gate** | The steps that wait for your yes | Any email sent to a stale deal — nothing goes out until you approve |
| **Deliverable** | Where the finished work lands | The summary in your Library, every Wednesday, whether or not you thought about it |

Notice what's missing from that table: you. No step involves you remembering, re-explaining, or checking whether the tool was connected. That absence is the entire point.

## How do you set one up?

The setup is one working session, and it reuses the brief you already wrote when you delegated the task manually:

1. **Pick the task.** The one you did most recently, most mechanically, and most resent. The Monday report is the usual first choice.
2. **Choose the trigger.** If it must happen by a certain day, use a schedule. If it should happen *whenever something occurs*, use an event trigger.
3. **Write the brief once — outcome, audience, constraints, check.** The four-part brief from our delegation playbook applies unchanged; a recurring brief is the same brief with a clock attached.
4. **Set the gate.** Decide which steps are sensitive — outbound sends, money, anything with outside impact — and mark them to wait for your approval.
5. **Run it once manually before the first scheduled run.** Watch the deliverable land, correct the brief if needed, and only then let the schedule take over.

Step 5 is the one people skip and shouldn't. You're auditing the automation before it ever runs unsupervised, not after it's already emailed a client something half-right.

## How do you keep it from doing something you'd regret?

Two mechanisms carry the trust load, and both are set at configuration time, not per run. First, the approval gate: anything with outside impact — sends, payments, deletions — pauses until you say go, so the worst case of an unattended run is a draft waiting in a queue, not an action already taken. Second, scoping: **~1,000 integrations connected day one, with per-agent tool scoping** — each colleague touches only the tools its role needs, so the colleague running your pipeline sweep sees your CRM, not your books or your inbox.

That combination is what makes "set once, runs weekly" defensible instead of reckless. You're not trusting an unsupervised system with your business; you're trusting a scoped colleague with one job, and keeping the irreversible steps behind a gate you control.

## Should this be a schedule or a trigger?

Most recurring work is obviously one or the other, but a few tasks sit on the line — here's the rule of thumb:

| If the work… | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Must happen on a known day, regardless of anything else | **Schedule** | Monday 9:00 growth report |
| Should happen the moment something occurs | **Event trigger** | New lead → research brief + first-draft outreach |
| Has a deadline but variable start | **Schedule + condition** | Friday 16:00 invoice chase — only unpaid invoices past 7 days |
| Depends on another team's output | **Chained trigger** | Meeting notes done → summary → follow-up drafts |

When in doubt, start with a schedule. It's predictable, easy to audit, and converts to a trigger later if the timing turns out to matter more than the clock.

## What does the first month look like?

Week one: automate one task — the report you dread most — and let it run twice. Week two: add a second, and check whether the first needed its brief corrected. Week three: convert one manual habit you didn't plan to automate, because by then you'll know what a good brief looks like. Week four: review the Library, not the calendar — the pile of finished deliverables is the proof the layer is working.

The pattern to resist is automating five things on day one. One automation that survives a month is worth five that needed rescuing in week two.
