# The Monday growth report, fully automated: an operator's playbook

Somewhere between your first coffee and your first meeting, Monday morning gets spent. You pull last week's numbers from Stripe, check the pipeline, count support tickets, and write it all up — and the week's real work starts at 11 instead of 9.

That report is the most automatable recurring work you own, because it never changes shape. Same sources, same sections, same questions, every week. This playbook sets it up once, as a recurring brief, and lets it run from then on.

## What does "fully automated" actually have to cover?

The test is simple: at 9:00 Monday, the finished report is already in your Library, and you did nothing to produce it. Anything short of that is a dashboard you still have to read and write up.

Three jobs hide inside the phrase "growth report." Gathering — pull the week's numbers from your CRM, your payments, your support desk. Comparing — set them against last week and whatever you were aiming at. Writing — a short document that says what changed and what to do about it. Most tools do one of the three well. The report still needs a person to stitch them together. That person is you, every Monday, unless the whole chain runs without you.

## The setup: one brief, then it recurs

You don't build this as a workflow. You brief it the way you'd brief a capable hire on their first week, and the hiring happens around the work.

1. **Brief the outcome, not the steps.** One message is the whole job description: "Every Monday at 9:00, write my growth report. Pull revenue and new customers from Stripe, pipeline changes from HubSpot, ticket volume from Intercom. Sections: revenue, pipeline, support, and one thing that needs a decision this week." If you'd have to explain it to a new employee anyway, you can brief it.
2. **Let the team assemble itself.** Mochi, your Chief of Staff, splits the brief into tasks and hands each to the right specialist. The numbers work goes to your Strategy Lead; if the report ends with outreach that needs drafting, the Sales & Growth Lead picks that up with the right tools already connected.
3. **Scope the tools.** Each colleague only touches what you allow. The reporting hire can read Stripe and HubSpot; they don't need your inbox. Scoping is a two-minute decision at the start and the reason you don't think about it after.
4. **Set the schedule once.** "Mon 9:00 — Growth report" is a saved schedule, not a task you re-create. It runs weekly without a trigger from you.
5. **Put the gate where it matters.** The report lands in your Library on its own — reading it is safe. But if the brief ends with "and send it to my investors," that send waits at an approval gate until you say go. Sensitive actions queue; routine ones don't.

Setup time is one honest conversation. After that, the report is not your Monday problem.

## What Monday morning looks like after

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| 8:30 | Logging into three tools to pull numbers | Report already in your Library |
| 9:30 | Writing up what you found | Reading what was found, deciding |
| 10:30 | Formatting, attaching, forwarding | Acting on the one flagged decision |

The report stops being a morning and becomes a page. On Kohai's example week, 24 deliverables ship and 31 hours get reclaimed — and ops and reporting alone account for 7 of those hours. That's the Monday report, the pipeline sweep, and the rest of the recurring paperwork, handed off.

One caveat worth stating plainly: the first automated report will be a draft, not a revelation. Read it, correct the emphasis, and reply with what you'd change — the way you would with a new hire. By week three it reads the way you would have written it, and by week four you've stopped checking.

## Where else the same pattern pays

The Monday report is one instance of a shape: recurring work with fixed inputs, a fixed deadline, and maybe one sensitive step.

- **The invoice chase.** "Fri 16:00 — Invoice chase — needs approval." The Operations Lead finds overdue invoices, drafts the nudge emails, and queues them. Nothing sends until you say go, because a dunning email written by software and read by your best client deserves a gate.
- **Support triage.** The Customer Support Lead works tickets around the clock, sorts them into what's urgent, what's routine, and what needs you, and hands you the short list instead of the queue.

For a solo operator, that's three jobs that never quite get done, now done by default. If you lead a small team, it's the same pattern per function: give each workstream a lead — Strategy, Sales, Support, Ops — without adding payroll, and keep the sensitive parts at the gate.

## What does running it cost?

Plans start at $29.99/month for 12,000 credits and one seat, with a 7-day free trial. A weekly report is a small fraction of a week's worth of work — you can size what a job costs in credits before you brief it, so the bill is never a surprise. If the report saves you two hours a Monday, the math doesn't need a spreadsheet.

## How do you stay in control once it runs without you?

You never handed over control — you handed over the typing. You can change the brief in one message, the same way you'd redirect a colleague. Scoped tools mean each hire touches only its lane. And anything that leaves the building — an invoice nudge, an investor send — sits at an approval gate until you say go.

That's the difference between automation you trust and automation you audit. Set it up this week, brief it once, and spend next Monday reading a finished report instead of writing one.
