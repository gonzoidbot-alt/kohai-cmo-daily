# What Open-Weight Models Actually Are (and Why Your Business Should Care)

An open-weight AI model is a language model whose trained parameters — its weights — are published for anyone to download, run, and host. DeepSeek, Kimi, and GLM are the best-known examples. If you're evaluating AI tools for a business that handles client data, the term matters for two practical reasons: who is allowed to learn from your data, and what you pay every month.

You don't need a technical background to evaluate this. You need three definitions and four questions. Here they are.

## What a model weight is

A language model is a very large file of numbers. Training is the process that produces those numbers: the model reads enormous amounts of text and adjusts its internal values until it can predict language well. Those final values are the weights. Everything the model "knows" is stored in them.

When a lab keeps its weights private, you can't run the model yourself. You can only rent access to it through the lab's interface, on the lab's terms, priced per use. When a lab publishes its weights, the opposite is true: anyone with the hardware — or with a provider that has the hardware — can run that exact model, and no single company controls who gets to use it.

That's the whole distinction. Open-weight means the trained file is public. Closed-weight means it isn't.

## What an inference provider is

A model by itself is useless to you — it's a file, not a service. Someone has to run it on GPUs, keep it fast and available, and expose it to the software you use. That someone is an inference provider.

Here's the part that changes your negotiating position: with an open-weight model, many providers can run the same model, because the file is public. They compete on price, uptime, and terms. With a closed-weight model, there is exactly one seller of access — the lab — and every platform that uses it pays that lab's price and accepts that lab's data terms.

So when an AI platform tells you which models it runs, you're learning two things at once: the capability you're buying, and how many companies stand between you and the bill.

## Why "no-training" is the phrase to watch

When your business data moves through a hosted model, one question decides most of the risk: is the provider allowed to use that data to train future versions of the model?

If they are, your client's contract terms, your financials, your drafts — all of it becomes raw material for a system other people use. Some providers' default terms reserve exactly that right unless you opt out, and the opt-out often carries conditions. For a business handling client data, that's not a footnote. It's a dealbreaker.

Open-weight providers compete on this. Because many providers can run the same published model, one that commits in writing not to train on your data can win your business without giving up the model you want. The commitment lives in a contract with a named company — not in a settings toggle you hope stays on. Kōhai runs exclusively on open-weight models — DeepSeek, Kimi, and GLM — from inference providers that certify they don't train on customer data, with per-tenant isolation and encryption underneath. The models are named and the commitment is written down. That's what makes the arrangement checkable rather than something you take on faith.

## What this does to your bill

The privacy story and the pricing story are the same story, viewed from two ends.

A platform running closed-weight models pays a per-use premium to a single seller, on terms that seller sets. A platform running open-weight models buys inference in a competitive market. When your vendor's input costs are competed rather than dictated, flat, predictable pricing becomes possible — and that shows up in what you're offered.

Kōhai's pricing is the direct example: **"Kōhai Pro is $100/month for 40,000 credits with unlimited team seats on every plan."** Unlimited seats matter because a closed-stack platform's per-seat pricing exists to recover the model premium on every additional user. When the model isn't the expensive part, the platform can stop charging you for the people and start charging you for the work. Credits map to the work itself — a small task runs 5–10 credits, a blog post or landing page runs 20–50, a full deck or research package runs 100–300. A failed task consumes zero credits. Add-on credits never expire.

Two budget rules follow, and they hold for any platform, not just this one:

1. **Price the work, not the seats.** If you're adding a teammate, ask what their workload costs per month — not what their login costs.
2. **Ask what a busy month costs.** The plan price is the floor. The honest number is the price at twice your expected volume.

## The four questions to ask any AI vendor

Whatever platform you're evaluating — including this one — these four questions separate vendors who can answer from vendors who improvise:

| Question | What a good answer contains |
|---|---|
| Which models run my work? | Named models, in one sentence, no "we use the best models" |
| Who runs them, and do they train on my data? | A named provider with a written no-training commitment |
| Where does my data sit? | Per-tenant isolation, encryption, and which staff can access it |
| What does the bill look like at 2x my usage? | A concrete number, not "it scales with your needs" |

Notice that open-weight platforms find the first two questions easy by construction: the models are public and the providers are named. A vendor who can't answer them in two sentences is telling you where their margin lives.

## Where this leaves you

Open-weight is not a feature. It's an architecture decision a platform made before you arrived, and it determines three things you care about: whether your data can become training material, how many providers compete for the work, and whether your bill stays legible as you grow.

You don't have to take any platform's word on it — including Kōhai's. Ask the four questions, read the answers, and prefer the vendor whose answers are written down.
