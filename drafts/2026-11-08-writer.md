# Your first AI colleague starts today. Here's what day one should look like.

Onboarding an AI employee takes one message, not a setup project. You brief the colleague the way you'd write a job description for a new hire — the outcome you want, not the steps to get there — and the first deliverable lands in your Library the same day. That's the whole test of the "hire, don't build" approach: if day one is configuration, you've bought a platform. If day one is work, you've hired a colleague.

Most founders never get to run that test, because most of the category is built the other way. This post walks through what the first day actually looks like when hiring is the model — and the one check worth running before you hand anything over.

## Why "starts today" is the whole test

When people evaluate AI tools, they compare features. That's reasonable, but it skips the cost that actually decides whether the tool gets used: time-to-first-value.

Build-first platforms have a setup phase measured in days or weeks. You map your process into a workflow, configure triggers, test the branches, and maintain all of it when reality changes shape. The tool promises leverage *eventually*. In the meantime, the work is still yours — plus the new work of building and maintaining the automation itself.

Hire-first platforms invert the order. The colleague already knows how to work; your job is to describe the outcome. The setup phase is one message, and the value phase starts when the reply lands. If a vendor's onboarding docs are longer than your first brief, that's a signal about which model they're really selling.

## What day one actually looks like

Here's the shape of a first day, in four steps:

1. **Write the brief.** One message. Not "set up a lead-generation workflow with enrichment stages" — that's build language. Hire language: "Find 50 e-commerce leads that match our ICP and draft the first-touch outreach for each." The outcome is the job description. The colleague figures out the steps.
2. **The colleague works.** It pulls from the tools it's scoped to — with around a thousand integrations connected from day one, the connective tissue is usually already there. It researches, drafts, and assembles while you do something else.
3. **The deliverable lands in your Library.** Not a chat transcript — a document, a spreadsheet, a deck. Finished work, filed where you can find it again, share it, and build on it. Chat is where work goes to evaporate; the Library is where it compounds.
4. **Sensitive actions wait at the approval gate.** If the work involves sending the outreach, publishing, or spending money, the colleague stops and asks. You approve or decline. This is the part founders expect to hate about AI delegation and usually end up liking most: you stay in control, and you mean it.

Then you adjust the brief and run it again. By the second cycle you're not onboarding anymore — you're managing, which is a skill you already have.

## The one check to run before you hand over context

Day one involves trust: you're giving a colleague access to your tools, your data, maybe your clients' data. Most buyers check permissions and stop there. Worth going one question deeper — what models is this running on, and who sees the data in transit?

It's a fair question to ask any vendor, and a good answer sounds like this: open-weight models only — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — from certified no-training providers. That's the Kohai stack, stated as a fact you can verify rather than a vibe you have to trust. Two follow-ups worth confirming while you're at it: per-agent tool scoping (your support colleague can touch the support inbox, not your bank feed), and tenant isolation with encryption, so your workspace's data isn't commingled with anyone else's.

None of this is privacy theater for its own sake. It's the difference between delegating *real* work — the kind with client names in it — and delegating only the safe-looking work. If the model stack can't be described plainly, that tells you something too.

## What if day one includes shipping something?

Some first briefs aren't documents. "Build a landing page for the workshop" or "make an internal tool for tracking refunds" are build-shaped requests. On hire-first platforms, that's still a brief, not a project: App Studio takes the request and ships real, deployed, hosted software — not a mockup you then have to hand to a developer. The deliverable is a URL, and it lands in the same Library as everything else.

This is where the hire frame earns its keep. The alternative — wiring up an automation platform until a page deploys — turns your first day into your first month.

## How to pick the first task

One practical rule: start with the task you did most recently and resented most. For solo operators that's usually lead research, first-draft content, or the weekly reporting grind. For lean teams it's often support triage or the Monday report.

Pick something with a clear done-state, keep the first brief small, and let the approval gate do its job on anything sensitive. The point of day one isn't to delegate your whole job. It's to prove the loop works — brief, deliverable, approve — so that by Friday the question isn't "can I trust this?" but "what do I hand over next?"
