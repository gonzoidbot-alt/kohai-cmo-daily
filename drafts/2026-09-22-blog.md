# Why your AI bill shouldn't be a surprise

Two founders can run almost identical AI workloads and end the month with bills that differ by 400%. Not because one got more done — because of how their platforms meter the work. One pays for the machinery: every token of every prompt, retry, and internal step. The other pays for the output.

The second model is easier to budget, easier to justify, and — this is the part most buyers miss — only possible when the underlying models don't carry frontier-API markup. Here's how to tell the difference before your card is on file.

## The two pricing models in this category

Nearly every AI-workforce product meters one of two things:

| Model | What you pay for | What the bill looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Token-based / usage pass-through | Raw computation: model choice, prompt length, retries, agent steps | Variable. Changes with your usage, your model, and the vendor's markup |
| Credit-based subscription | Finished work: a deliverable costs a published range of credits | Fixed monthly. You know the ceiling before the month starts |

Token-based pricing isn't a scam — it's honest about costs. But it moves the meter to your side of the glass. You're charged for computation you don't directly control: an agent that re-reads a long document three times costs three times as much, and you find out at month-end.

Credit-based pricing puts the abstraction on the vendor. The platform absorbs the variance of running models efficiently, and you price the *work* instead of the *computation*.

## What credits actually cost per unit of work

The concrete version, from Kohai's own economics: **"First AI hire: $29.99/month, 12,000 credits, 7-day free trial."**

Per 02-positioning-icp.md, a blog post runs 20–50 credits and a deck runs 100–300. At that rate, a solo plan's 12,000 monthly credits cover roughly 240–600 blog posts or 40–120 decks. You don't need to model your usage in a spreadsheet — you can see what a deliverable costs before you brief it, and the plan price caps the month.

That's the whole mental shift. Stop asking "what will my token spend be?" and start asking "how many deliverables do I need this month?" The first question requires an engineering forecast. The second is just your to-do list.

## The five questions that expose unpredictable pricing

Before you subscribe to any AI-workforce platform, ask these. Evasive answers are the finding.

1. **What does one deliverable cost?** If the vendor can't translate their meter into "a blog post costs X," you can't budget. Credit-per-asset ranges should be published, not reverse-engineered.
2. **What happens when I run out?** The honest answers are "you pause" or "you top up knowingly." The bad answer is "you keep going and see the bill" — overage without a gate is how usage pricing surprises people.
3. **What am I charged for that isn't the deliverable?** Retries, agent reasoning steps, failed runs. Some platforms bill the scaffolding. You're buying the document, not the scaffolding.
4. **Do credits roll over or expire?** Neither answer is wrong, but silence on it is a red flag — it means the vendor would rather you didn't ask.
5. **What's the cheapest month possible?** A fixed subscription has a floor: your plan price, full stop. Usage-based pricing has no floor because the meter never sleeps.

## Why credit pricing at $29.99 is even possible

Here's the structural reason most platforms can't offer a small, predictable plan: their cost of goods is someone else's frontier API, marked up. When every token you run carries a resale margin, the only way to make the unit economics work is to charge per token — which pushes variance onto you.

Kohai takes a different route. **"Open-weight models only — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — from certified no-training providers."** Running exclusively on open-weight models removes the per-token resale margin from the cost structure entirely, which is what makes a $29.99 plan with 12,000 credits possible without gambling on your usage staying low. The privacy story and the pricing story are the same story: the model economics are ours to manage, not billed through to you.

And the certification matters as much as the weights. "Certified no-training providers" means your documents, client data, and drafts don't become someone's training set — so the predictable bill doesn't come at the price of feeding your business into someone else's model.

## What predictable pricing changes about how you delegate

This is the underrated effect. When a deliverable has a published credit cost, you brief differently — you delegate more, not less. Handing off the Friday invoice chase stops being a "what will this cost me in API calls" calculation and becomes a normal management decision, the same one you'd make about giving a task to a person.

Unpredictable pricing has the opposite effect. Founders watching a usage meter rationally ration their own delegation — they save the AI for the "important" asks and keep doing the repetitive work themselves, which is exactly the work they bought the tool to offload. A bill you can't predict doesn't just cost money; it costs usage.

So the pricing model isn't a billing detail. It determines whether the tool becomes part of how your business runs or stays a toy you're cautious with.

## A simple test this week

If you already use an AI tool with usage pricing, pull last month's bill and try to explain a single line item. If you can't — if the bill is a stack of computations you never consciously chose — that's the unpredictability tax, and it compounds.

If you're evaluating new tools, run the five questions above. The ones with published credit costs, a hard plan ceiling, and a trial period are the ones built for operators. The ones that hedge are built for metering.

Kohai's 7-day free trial covers the whole experiment: brief one recurring task, watch the deliverable land in your Library, and know exactly what it cost before the trial ends.
