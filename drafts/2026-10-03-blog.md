# How many AI credits do you actually need?

Enough to cover the recurring work you'd actually delegate — and you can know that number before you pick a plan. A blog post runs 20–50 credits, a deck 100–300, and every other task quotes its price before it runs. This post is the arithmetic that turns those per-task prices into a monthly budget.

That arithmetic matters because "how many credits" is usually answered with a shrug. Plan tables list totals; the buyer is left guessing whether their month fits. It doesn't have to be a guess.

## What does one task cost in credits?

Kohai publishes the price of the work, not just the plan: a blog post runs 20–50 credits, a deck 100–300. Short written work sits at the low end of that scale; assembled deliverables sit higher, because they take more work to produce.

You rarely need to memorize a price list, though. The cost of a task is quoted before you brief it — the quote arrives with the assignment, not on the invoice. The published ranges are there to sanity-check the quotes; the per-brief quoting is what makes a month plannable.

It also changes how you think about assigning work. When a price shows up before the job starts, you make the same call you'd make with any vendor: worth it, or not this month. The decision moves to where your judgment is, instead of hiding until the bill lands.

## How do you turn task prices into a monthly budget?

List the recurring work first, the way you'd write a grocery list — the pieces that happen every week whether or not you feel like them. Then price each one and multiply by how often it runs:

| Recurring work | Monthly volume | Published credit range | Monthly total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blog posts | 8 | 20–50 | 160–400 |
| Decks | 2 | 100–300 | 200–600 |
| Ops work (reports, follow-ups, summaries) | weekly | quoted per brief | counted as quoted |

The two published rows land between 360 and 1,000 credits a month in this example — your volumes will differ, but the shape won't: every line on the list is countable, and every line not on the list quotes itself when you brief it. That's the difference between a budget and a meter. A meter tells you what you spent after you spent it; a budget tells you what the month will cost while you're still deciding what to assign.

It's also how the work grows honestly. When a new recurring task appears — a client newsletter, a weekly hiring update — it joins the list with its own quoted price, and the budget moves by a number you can see. Nothing about the month gets less predictable because one more line was added.

## What does a full week of briefed work look like?

The published reference point: "Kohai's example week: 24 deliverables shipped, 31 hours reclaimed." Twenty-four finished pieces in a week tells you something about scale — individual tasks stay small enough to price, and the capacity adds up in the aggregate.

Notice what the claim counts: deliverables, not messages. Finished work in your Library — a report you can send, a deck you can present, a follow-up that actually went out. The 31 reclaimed hours are the part no meter measures: the week you didn't spend being the machine that produces those 24 pieces.

For a founder, that week is also a sizing sample. If a busy week of briefed work produces 24 deliverables, then a month's budget is built from the quieter version of that week — the recurring pieces you'd assign every time, priced one by one. The example week doesn't set your budget; it tells you the work arrives in pieces small enough to have prices at all.

## How do you size a plan before you commit?

Run your real week on the 7-day free trial and read the totals off the trial, not off a plan table. One measured week of your actual recurring work — the reports you really send, the follow-ups you really chase — is worth more than any sizing guide, including this one.

Two sizing errors cancel each other out this way. Sizing from your best day underbuys, because launch weeks aren't average weeks. Sizing from fear overbuys, because headroom you never assign is just money parked. The trial number sits between them, and it's yours.

## The budget you can defend

An AI budget you can defend to yourself — or to a budget holder — is a list of work with prices attached, not a meter with a mood. Write the list, brief the week, and let the trial tell you what your month actually weighs.

If the measured number fits a plan with room to grow, you've found your answer. If it doesn't, you've learned that before the invoice did — which is the entire point of credits.
