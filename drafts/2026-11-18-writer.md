# The weekly client report is the task nobody owns. Hand it over.

Every agency has a Thursday. The work of the week is done — campaigns ran, tickets closed, projects moved — and now one person sits down with five browser tabs and a spreadsheet to prove it. Numbers get copied out of dashboards, screenshots get pasted into a deck, the deck gets renamed three times, and the "weekly client report" that was supposed to take an hour eats the evening. It goes out late anyway.

If you run accounts at a small agency or a lean professional-services team, you know this report is not hard work. It's *repetitive* work — which is a different problem, and one with a different fix.

This is a playbook for handing the weekly client report to an AI teammate. Not an assistant that drafts when you ask — a standing workflow that runs on a schedule, produces the report in your Library, and waits for your approval before anything reaches the client.

## Why the client report keeps getting deferred

The report has no natural owner. Whoever did the work that week is in the next engagement by Thursday. Compiling what happened requires touching every tool the week touched — the ad platform, the ticket queue, the project board, the analytics account — and nobody's calendar has a slot labeled "assemble evidence."

So the report inherits the fate of every recurring task without an owner: it slides. A week late, two weeks late. The client doesn't complain — they just start reading your agency as slightly less accountable than the one that reports on time. That's the real cost. Not the hour, the drift.

The fix is structural: the report needs a standing owner who never gets pulled into another project.

## The playbook, in five steps

**1. Brief the colleague once.** You don't build a workflow; you write the outcome the way you'd write it for a capable hire: "Every Friday at 3pm, pull this client's ad spend, site traffic, ticket stats, and project status from last week, and draft our standard weekly report — what we shipped, the numbers, blockers, and what's next." One message is the whole job description.

**2. Give it scoped access to the tools the week lives in.** Kohai connects to around 1,000 of the apps your team already uses on day one, with per-agent tool scoping — the teammate working on Client A's report sees Client A's tools, not the whole shop. Set the access once; it holds across every week after.

**3. Set the schedule.** Friday, 3pm, every week. This is the part that makes it a playbook and not a trick: the schedule persists without you maintaining it. The colleague doesn't need reminding, doesn't go on holiday, and doesn't get reassigned.

**4. Read what lands in your Library.** The report shows up the way a document from a colleague does — not a chat transcript you have to reformat. What shipped, what moved, the numbers, open blockers, next week's plan. Where a section needs your judgment — an interpretation of why traffic dipped, say — it says so, rather than papering over the gap.

**5. Approve before it goes anywhere.** Anything sensitive — the send itself, any client-facing claim — waits at an approval gate until you say go. You stay in control, and that's not a disclaimer; it's the design. The report the teammate produces is a draft for an account owner who actually knows the client. You read it in three minutes, add the one human line that matters ("heads up, we're shifting budget to X next month"), and send.

That's the whole loop. Brief once, scope the tools, schedule it, read, approve.

## What it frees up

On the site's own example workspace — a fully staffed week — reporting and ops account for about 7 reclaimed hours a week, and the whole example week ships 24 deliverables and reclaims 31 hours. Those are example figures, not a case study; treat them as the shape of a staffed week, not a promise. But the shape is the point: the reporting hour is exactly the kind of work that compounds quietly. Every week you don't write the report by hand is an hour that goes back to the work only your team can do — the client conversation, the strategy, the judgment call.

## The question every agency should ask first

Before handing any AI tool access to client accounts, there's a question worth asking out loud: where does this data go? Most platforms run on frontier models whose providers may train on your inputs — which, for an agency working under NDAs and client agreements, is not a detail. It's the whole reason to say no.

It's the reason to say yes to this stack, too: *"Open-weight models only — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — from certified no-training providers."* Inference providers are certified not to train on your data, and work is isolated per tenant with encryption. Your client's numbers go in, the report comes out, and the data doesn't become anyone else's training material. For an agency, that's not a compliance footnote — it's the precondition for running the playbook at all.

## Start with one client

Don't roll this out across every account on Monday. Pick your most template-driven report — the one you've written forty times — brief it on Thursday, and let the first draft land in your Library before the weekend. If it reads like something you'd send with one edit, schedule it. If it doesn't, the brief needs work, and fixing a brief takes ten minutes — not the five weeks it takes to hire the human equivalent.

Either way, you get Thursday night back. That's the trade.
