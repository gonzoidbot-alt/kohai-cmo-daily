# You don't want an agent. You want a colleague.

A few weeks ago we argued that the fastest way to drown in AI tooling is to start building agents. The response we keep hearing is a fair one: "I get that building is work. But what's the alternative, exactly?"

The alternative has a name, and it isn't "agent." It's "colleague." The difference sounds like semantics until you look at what each one asks of you — and what each one hands back.

## The real cost of being your own agent-architect

When you build an agent, the product you're making isn't the growth report or the outreach sequence. The product is the agent itself: the trigger, the prompt, the tool connections, the error branch for when the tool changes its response format. That's a software project, and like every software project, it ships with a maintenance contract.

Here's the part nobody puts on the builder-tool landing pages. The build is the cheap half. The expensive half is that every agent you own becomes a small system you're the sysadmin for. Prompts drift as your business changes. Integrations update their APIs. An agent that worked in March silently produces worse output in August, and the only person positioned to notice is the person who built it. You.

This isn't a knock on the builder tools — platforms like Lindy and Relevance AI make genuinely capable builders, and if constructing workflows is the work you enjoy, they're good at it. It's a knock on the premise. The tools make building easier, not optional. You're still the architect, the QA department, and the on-call engineer. For a founder whose actual job is sales, content, and keeping customers, that's a second job with no salary and no finish line.

## Colleague vs. agent: what actually changes

The distinction isn't marketing. It shows up in four places, and each one removes a chunk of that second job.

| | AI agent (build-it-yourself) | AI colleague (hire-once) |
|---|---|---|
| How it starts | You design a workflow and write the prompt | You write one brief — one message is the whole job description |
| Who assembles the work | You wire the steps together | The team assembles itself — a Chief of Staff splits your brief across specialists |
| What you get back | Intermediate output you inspect and finish | Finished deliverables, in your Library |
| When it breaks | You debug the prompt and the plumbing | You re-brief, the way you'd redirect a hire |

Read the right-hand column again and notice what's missing: you. Not from the loop — you stay in it, at the approval gate, where anything sensitive waits for your go. You're missing from the plumbing. The plumbing is someone else's job now.

That's the whole thesis in one line: an agent is a thing you operate. A colleague is a person you'd recognize on a team — named, scoped to the tools you allow, and briefed rather than programmed.

## What day one looks like for a colleague

On Kohai, the sequence runs like this. You describe an outcome — "find 50 e-commerce leads and draft the outreach." Mochi, your Chief of Staff, splits that into tasks and hands each to the right specialist — the Sales & Growth Lead picks up the prospecting with the right tools already connected, from a library of about 1,000 integrations. Sensitive steps, like actually sending the outreach, queue at an approval gate until you say go. Finished work — docs, decks, spreadsheets — lands in your Library.

No workflow diagram existed at any point in that sequence. No prompt was engineered. If the output isn't right, you don't open a builder — you say what to change, the way you would to a capable new hire on their first week.

And the economics hold up against the hours you'd spend building. "First AI hire: $29.99/month, 12,000 credits, 7-day free trial." Credits, not tokens — a blog post runs 20–50 credits, a deck 100–300, so you can see what a piece of work costs before you assign it. No surprise bill at the end of a busy month, which is its own kind of trust.

## "But I like building"

Some founders genuinely do. If wiring an n8n graph on a Saturday morning is your version of relaxing, keep the hobby — we mean that. The builder crowd isn't wrong, they're just building for a different customer: the operator who wants tooling as a craft.

The person this post is for is the one who started building an agent because a blog post said they should, and now has eleven half-finished workflows, a prompt library they're scared to touch, and a Monday report they still write by hand. For that person, building was never the goal. Finished work was.

## The ask, restated

Stop asking "which agent builder should I learn?" and start asking "which role on my week do I want to hand off first?" The Monday report. The invoice chase. The pipeline sweep. Pick one, brief it like a colleague, and judge the result by a single standard: did finished work land without you operating anything?

"Kohai's example week: 24 deliverables shipped, 31 hours reclaimed." That's a week where the reports got written, the follow-ups got sent, and the founder's calendar had room in it. That's what the colleague is for.
