# The automation you built is a second job. Hire a colleague instead

You spent a weekend wiring it up: the trigger, the steps, the model call, the branch for when the model gets it wrong. For two weeks it ran. Then the API changed a field name, or a step started failing silently, or the output drifted and nobody noticed until a customer did. Now the thing that was supposed to save you time gets a calendar block, because you are its maintenance plan.

If that's where you are, the problem isn't your building skills. It's that you built machinery when what you needed was a hire. Machinery needs an operator. A hire needs a brief.

## Why do built agent workflows keep breaking?

Because you own every layer. The connector to your CRM, the prompt text, the retry logic, the error path, the monitoring that tells you it broke — all of it is yours, and all of it rots. A workflow with no owner isn't leverage; it's a slow leak of attention.

The maintenance is also the invisible part of the price. A built workflow costs you the weekend to create and then a slice of every week after, forever. Most founders never add that second number up. When they do, the build path stops looking free.

There's a quieter cost too: every hour you spend as the on-call engineer for your own tooling is an hour not spent on the work only you can do — selling, shipping, deciding. The build path doesn't just fail. It fails slowly, in the currency you have least of.

## What does hiring an AI colleague actually mean?

You brief it like a colleague, not configure it like software. One message is the whole job description: "Find 50 e-commerce leads and draft the outreach." No flowchart, no node graph, no branch conditions. The colleague decides the steps, uses the tools already connected to your workspace, and the finished work — the spreadsheet, the document, the draft — lands in your Library for review.

The difference is who owns the machinery:

| | You built it | You hired it |
|---|---|---|
| Setup | A weekend of wiring | A brief in plain language |
| When an API changes | You debug it | It's handled for you |
| New kind of task | Build another workflow | Brief the same colleague |
| Your ongoing role | Operator and on-call | Reviewer and approver |

That last row is the real shift. With a build, your job description quietly grew. With a hire, it doesn't: you describe outcomes and review work, which is the job you actually wanted.

Kohai's roles are named like a team for exactly this reason — Strategy Lead for metrics and dashboards, Sales & Growth Lead for prospects and sequences, Marketing & Comms Lead for content, Customer Support Lead for the ticket queue, Operations Lead for reporting and automation. You can read that list and know instantly which one takes the work you're doing at 9pm. Try picturing "Workflow #4: lead-gen-v3-FINAL" the same way.

## What about the parts you wouldn't hand over blind?

This is where the colleague model differs from the autonomy pitch you've heard before. Sensitive actions don't just happen. Anything that touches real money, real people, or real data waits at an approval gate until you say go. You stay in control — not as a slogan, but as a design decision: the colleague works continuously, and you approve the steps that matter.

So the handover isn't all-or-nothing. Start with the work you'd happily never touch again: the Monday report, the invoice chase, the support queue at 2am. Keep the gates on anything consequential. Widen the delegation as trust is earned by delivered work, not by a settings toggle.

## What does it cost to make the switch?

Less than the weekend you lost to the last breakage. Start on Kōhai Free — $0, no card, for a limited time — and your first AI teammates are working today. After the free period, plans run $20, $100, and $200 a month, with unlimited seats on every plan — so the whole team of roles is included whether you're a team of one or fifteen.

Credits are priced per task, and a task quotes its price before it runs. A blog post runs 20–50 credits; a deck runs 100–300; a failed task consumes zero. You can see what a month of delegation costs before the month happens — no surprise bill, no per-seat math to defend to anyone.

## What does the work look like when it comes back?

Finished, and in your Library. Kōhai's example week: 24 deliverables shipped, 31 hours reclaimed. That's an example week, not a promise — but it's the shape of the trade: the recurring work that filled your calendar comes back as reviewed deliverables, and the hours go to the decisions only you can make.

The agent you built was a project. A colleague you hired is staff. When the next API change lands, one of those notices and adapts, and the other one is you, again, on a Sunday.
