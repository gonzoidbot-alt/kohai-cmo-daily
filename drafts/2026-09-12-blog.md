# How to choose an AI workforce platform: six checks before you sign up

The AI-employee category has grown fast enough that "platform" now means ten different things. Some products are workflow builders with a hiring metaphor. Some are chat assistants with a roster. A few actually do the work and hand you finished deliverables.

The difference shows up in your first week, not on the pricing page. So here are six checks to run before you commit — the same questions we'd want asked of us, and of anyone else in the category.

## Do you want to build workflows or get work done?

This is the first fork, and it decides most of the rest. Builder-style tools — Lindy, Relevance AI, n8n, and their peers — give you canvases, nodes, and prompts. They're genuinely good at what they do, and if you enjoy being the architect, they're the right shape. But they make you the integration layer: every handoff, every prompt, every fix is yours.

An AI workforce platform takes the opposite brief: you describe the outcome, and a team assembles itself around the task. On Kohai, one message — "find 50 e-commerce leads and draft the outreach" — is the whole job description. Your Chief of Staff, Mochi, splits the goal and hands each piece to the right specialist. If the question "who maintains this when a step breaks?" makes you tired, that's your answer about which fork you're on.

## What does finished work actually look like?

Ask any platform to show you the end state, not the chat. Where does the work land? Is it a deliverable you can forward — a doc, a spreadsheet, a deck — or a text answer you have to reformat?

The bar we hold ourselves to: work arrives in your Library as a finished artifact. And when the ask is software, it should be real software. App Studio ships deployed software, not mockups — you can ask for a landing page or an internal tool and get something built, deployed, and hosted, not a screenshot of what it might look like someday. If a platform's "app builder" stops at a prototype, that's a fair thing to know before you're counting on it.

## How do the integrations work on day one?

A workforce that can't reach your tools is a very expensive notepad. Three things to check:

| Check | What good looks like |
|---|---|
| Time to connected | Tools work on day one, not after a setup weekend |
| Breadth | Enough coverage that the work doesn't detour through you |
| Scoping | You decide which teammate touches which tool |

Kohai connects around 1,000 integrations on day one, with per-agent tool scoping — each colleague only touches what you allow. The scoping row matters more than the breadth row. "Access to everything" is a red flag dressed up as convenience.

## Where does the trust boundary sit?

Every AI platform now says some version of "you stay in control." The question is what that means mechanically. Two designs dominate:

1. **Audit-after.** The system acts, and you review logs. Control is a forensic exercise.
2. **Gate-before.** Sensitive actions wait for your explicit go. Control is a checkpoint.

The second is how you'd run a real hire: nobody sends the invoice-chase email or the outbound sequence without a sign-off the first time. On Kohai, sensitive actions wait at an approval gate until you say go — the report gets delivered, the email that could embarrass you waits in a queue. If a platform's pitch is full autonomy as the dream, read that as a design choice about whose weekend absorbs the mistakes.

## Is the pricing predictable, or is it a guess?

Two failure modes show up on AI invoices: usage you can't forecast, and seats that punish a small team. Per-token billing ties your bill to how verbose a model felt on a Tuesday. Task-based pricing ties it to the work.

Kohai prices in credits — a blog post runs 20–50, a deck 100–300 — so you can size a job before you brief it. Plans start at $29.99/mo for 12,000 credits and one seat, with a 7-day free trial; the Pro tier is $99.99/mo for 40,000 credits and 3 seats. The check to run on any platform: can you compute, before you commit, what your typical week of work will cost? If the answer is "submit the form and we'll talk," that's a pricing model that expects to surprise you.

## Whose hands does your data end up in?

Last check, and for some buyers the first one. If you handle client data — finance, legal, healthcare-adjacent work — ask two questions of every vendor: what happens to the work in transit, and does it become training data? "Our data trains your model" is a dealbreaker wearing a friendly font.

Kohai runs exclusively on open-weight models — DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM — from inference providers certified not to train on your data, with per-tenant isolation and encryption. That sentence is written to be forwarded to whoever asks the question. Whatever platform you choose, you should be able to write its version of that sentence in one line. If it takes a security questionnaire and a sales call to find out, keep looking.

## A quick scorecard

Summarized, the six checks:

1. Build vs. done — who maintains the middle?
2. Output — finished deliverables in a Library, or chat to reformat?
3. Integrations — day-one connections with per-agent scoping?
4. Trust — gate-before on sensitive actions, or audit-after?
5. Pricing — task-based credits you can forecast, or usage roulette?
6. Data — one-line privacy answer, or a questionnaire?

Run all six on any shortlist, generously — most vendors are honest about their shapes, and the right choice depends on which fork you're on. If your answers trend toward "get work done, finished deliverables, gates before autonomy, prices I can predict," that's the product Kohai is built to be, and the 7-day trial is there so you can verify the checklist instead of trusting it.
